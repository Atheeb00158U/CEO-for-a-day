# CEO For A Day: proposed syllabus

Research draft, 30 Oct 2026. Covers learning modules first, then Hot Seats as practice.

## How this was built

Business school strategy courses mostly teach the same topics: industry forces, positioning, competitive advantage, scale and scope. Where they differ is in how they teach them. Startup courses (YC Startup School, Stanford's Startup Garage, Lean LaunchPad) start somewhere else. They begin with customers and products, then cover growth, sales and money. A strong strategist needs both. Our players are Indian founders who are just starting or are struggling, so the order below starts with what they meet on Monday morning: cash, customers and price. Strategy comes in the middle of the course. Moats, disruption and bets come last.

My calls:

- **Money comes before strategy.** Most small Indian businesses don't die from bad strategy. They die from cash: udhaar (credit) they can't collect, thin margins they don't notice, and debt. Rumelt and Helmer are no use to someone who can't tell profit from cash.
- **Each lesson teaches one rule in one sentence and shows one rupee example.** Frameworks (5 Forces, 7 Powers, the kernel) are taught as tools after the player has met the problem they solve, not as lists to learn by heart.
- **Indian context is how we teach, not an extra module.** Kirana credit, GST, sachets, cash on delivery, family partners, "too costly" bargaining and UPI all show up as the examples inside ordinary lessons.
- **Thinking in Bets is the course's spine.** Duke's "resulting" (judging a call by how it turned out) is already how our feedback works: a choice is good or bad on its reasoning, and other outcomes are labelled as estimates. The idea is taught once in Unit 1 and reused everywhere after that.

Notation: lessons are numbered `U.M.L` (unit, module, lesson). Each line reads **principle**, then *example*.

## 1. Course structure

### Unit 1: How a business makes money (basics)

**Module 1.1: Money in, money out**
- 1.1.1 **Sales are not profit. Profit is what is left after every cost.** *You sold ₹2 lakh of sarees at the Diwali mela but spent ₹1.9 lakh on stock, stall and travel.*
- 1.1.2 **Know your margin on every item.** *A ₹10 biscuit pack earns the kirana ₹1, but a ₹10 loose namkeen packet earns ₹4. Which one gets the front shelf?*
- 1.1.3 **Fixed costs stay the same each month. Variable costs grow with every sale.** *Shop rent is fixed. Paper bags and delivery petrol are variable.*
- 1.1.4 **Break-even is the number of sales that pays all your fixed costs.** *Your tiffin service needs 60 meals a day just to pay rent and the cook.*
- 1.1.5 **Unit economics: one customer must earn you more than it costs to get them.** *You pay ₹300 in Instagram ads to win a customer who buys once for ₹400 at a ₹100 margin.*

**Module 1.2: Cash is king**
- 1.2.1 **Profit on paper can still leave an empty cash box.** *Your wholesale buyer pays in 90 days, but your supplier wants cash today.*
- 1.2.2 **Udhaar is a loan you give, and you must manage it like one.** *Regular customers owe your kirana ₹80,000. Set limits and a collection day.*
- 1.2.3 **Stock sitting on a shelf is cash you can't spend.** *Too many sizes of school shoes are lying unsold in May.*
- 1.2.4 **Keep a cash buffer for bad months.** *Monsoon cuts walk-in sales in half for six weeks.*

**Module 1.3: Rules of the game**
- 1.3.1 **Tax is a cost, so plan for it. Don't hide from it.** *GST composition lets a small trader pay a flat 1% on turnover, but then you can't collect GST, claim input credit, or sell to other states. ([ClearTax](https://cleartax.in/s/composition-scheme-under-gst); check current limits.)*
- 1.3.2 **Write down every rupee. You can't fix what you don't measure.** *A daily UPI statement plus a cash book shows where money leaks.*
- 1.3.3 **A good decision can have a bad result. Judge the thinking, not the luck.** *You stocked umbrellas and the rain came late. The bet was still sound.* (Thinking in Bets: "resulting".)

### Unit 2: Customers first

**Module 2.1: Find a real problem**
- 2.1.1 **Sell what people already struggle with, not what you love making.** *You love fusion cakes, but your area wants eggless birthday cakes on time.*
- 2.1.2 **Ask about their life, not your idea.** *Don't ask "Would you buy my app?" Ask "How did you book a plumber last time?"* (The Mom Test, rule 1.)
- 2.1.3 **Ask about the past. Don't ask about the future.** *"What did you spend on tuition last month?" beats "Would you pay for tuition?"* (Mom Test, rule 2.)
- 2.1.4 **Praise is not proof. Money or a firm promise is.** *Ten friends say "great idea", but zero of them pay an advance.*

**Module 2.2: Pick your customer**
- 2.2.1 **You can't serve everyone. Choose who you serve first.** *A gym for working women at 6 am, not "a gym for everybody".*
- 2.2.2 **Build for the biggest real need, not for the loudest buyer.** *The army wants jeeps, but millions of families want a small car.*
- 2.2.3 **Change your product to fit the customer. Don't try to change the customer.** *Buyers want 22-karat gold. Don't lecture them about 18-karat.*
- 2.2.4 **Follow your customers' new habits, even if your old income depends on the old ones.** *Customers now order on WhatsApp instead of walking in.*

**Module 2.3: What they really buy**
- 2.3.1 **People buy a result, not a product.** *Parents don't buy tuition. They buy "my child passes Class 10".*
- 2.3.2 **Feelings count as much as features.** *A family car is also a sign of status, so nobody wants to be seen in "the cheapest car" (Tata Nano).*
- 2.3.3 **Indian buyers care about value, not just a low price.** *They'll pay ₹50 more for a pressure cooker that lasts ten years.*

### Unit 3: Price and offer

**Module 3.1: Setting a price**
- 3.1.1 **Price from the value to the customer, not only from your cost.** *A same-day AC repair is worth more in May than in December.*
- 3.1.2 **Value goes up with a bigger result and more trust. It goes down with more waiting and more effort.** *A "fitted in 2 hours or free" curtain service beats a cheaper one that takes a week.* (Hormozi's value equation.)
- 3.1.3 **When a customer says "too costly", find out "compared to what?"** *They're comparing your ₹1,200 haircut with the ₹150 salon. Show what's different, or you're selling to the wrong person.*
- 3.1.4 **A small pack opens a big market.** *Chik sold shampoo at ₹1 a sachet to people who could never buy a bottle.*
- 3.1.5 **A low-priced entry item brings people through the door.** *Tanishq sold pieces from ₹399 so a big brand felt open to everyone.*

**Module 3.2: Discounts and price changes**
- 3.2.1 **Discounts can hide a product problem. They don't fix it.** *You cut prices every month, and your sweets still don't sell.*
- 3.2.2 **Cutting the price of a premium product makes it look cheap.** *A "50% off" sign on your handloom range tells buyers it wasn't worth the full price.*
- 3.2.3 **Raise prices slowly and with warning. A sudden jump breaks trust.** *Tell your tuition parents in March, not on fee day.*
- 3.2.4 **When demand is far above supply, add supply before raising the price.** *A two-year waiting list feeds a black market and resentment.*
- 3.2.5 **Remove the cost of switching to you.** *"Bring your old gold, we'll test it and exchange it fairly." Pay to solve the buyer's problem.*

### Unit 4: Trust, brand and selling

**Module 4.1: Trust is the product in India**
- 4.1.1 **Take the risk off the buyer.** *Flipkart's cash on delivery let people pay only when the parcel arrived.*
- 4.1.2 **Let customers check your promise for themselves.** *Free gold testing in the store: proof beats a slogan.*
- 4.1.3 **Keep promises in bad times too. That is when people remember.** *You still take your farmers' milk in the winter glut.*
- 4.1.4 **When trust is low, keep things simple and speak plainly.** *After a billing mistake, send one clear message, not three new plans.*

**Module 4.2: Why people say yes** (Cialdini)
- 4.2.1 **Reciprocity: give first, and people want to give back.** *A free tasting at the sweet shop.*
- 4.2.2 **Social proof: people follow what others do.** *"500 families in this colony use us."*
- 4.2.3 **Authority and liking: people trust experts and people they like.** *The pharmacist who remembers your mother's medicines.*
- 4.2.4 **Scarcity and commitment: rare things feel valuable, and a small yes leads to a bigger yes.** *A ₹99 trial class before the ₹4,999 course.*
- 4.2.5 **Unity: people buy from "one of us".** *A farmers' co-op brand, or "made in our town".*
- 4.2.6 **Use these to help a true promise land, never to trick.** *Fake "only 2 left" timers get you reported and kill repeat sales.*

**Module 4.3: Brand**
- 4.3.1 **People can only pick a brand they remember.** *Good ghee with no name loses to a famous name on the shelf.*
- 4.3.2 **Sell what you stand for, not just specs.** *"Pure, like home" beats "99.7% fat content".*
- 4.3.3 **Being correct is not the same as being remembered. Give your brand a voice.** *Amul's topical ads every week.*
- 4.3.4 **Word of mouth and teaching for free can beat paid ads.** *Zerodha grew mostly through referrals and free education, with little advertising.*

### Unit 5: Running the business well (operations)

**Module 5.1: Simple wins**
- 5.1.1 **Every extra variety adds hidden costs.** *40 menu items mean 40 sets of raw stock, waste and training.* (LEGO cut its pieces.)
- 5.1.2 **One standard way of working lowers cost and errors.** *IndiGo flew one plane type, so it needed one set of spares and one kind of pilot training.*
- 5.1.3 **Do a few things really well before adding more.** *Master dosa before you add Chinese and pizza.*

**Module 5.2: Quality, suppliers and speed**
- 5.2.1 **Pick a few strong suppliers on merit, and push them hard.** *Two reliable fabric mills, not twelve cheap ones.*
- 5.2.2 **Keep your core skill in your own hands.** *Outsource packing, never the recipe.*
- 5.2.3 **On time with a plan beats perfect and late.** *Open for the wedding season with bought-in parts, and promise local within a year.*
- 5.2.4 **Test a risky change in one place before you roll it out.** *Try "no delivery charge" in one area for a month first.*
- 5.2.5 **Buy what already works instead of waiting for an overdue project.** *Use a ready billing app rather than a custom one that is 18 months late.*

**Module 5.3: Building a team**
- 5.3.1 **A business that needs you for every decision can't grow.** *If the shop shuts when you're at a family wedding, you own a job, not a business.* (Talreja's "a business which can grow without them".)
- 5.3.2 **Write down how things are done so others can do them.** *A one-page checklist for opening the store.*
- 5.3.3 **Hire for attitude and ownership, train for skill.** *The helper who counts stock without being told.*
- 5.3.4 **Family and partners need written roles and an exit plan.** *Two brothers, one shop and no agreement means a fight at the next generation.*

### Unit 6: Money, debt and risk

**Module 6.1: Where money comes from**
- 6.1.1 **Every rupee you raise has a price, in interest or in control.** *A bank loan costs 14%. An investor costs 20% of your company.*
- 6.1.2 **Borrow only against cash you are sure will come in.** *Don't take a loan for a new branch on hopeful sales.*
- 6.1.3 **Rent what you only need to use, and own what makes you special.** *Lease the delivery vans. Own the kitchen recipe.* (IndiGo's sale-and-leaseback.)
- 6.1.4 **Never use money that isn't yours to make your own bets.** *Customer advances are not your trading capital.* (Harshad Mehta.)

**Module 6.2: Staying alive**
- 6.2.1 **Take money off the table when things are going very well.** *Pay back the loan in the boom, not after the crash.*
- 6.2.2 **In a slump, shrink fast and early.** *Close the weak outlet now, not after six more months of losses.*
- 6.2.3 **Cut the past, keep funding the future.** *Close the old product line but keep the new website budget.*
- 6.2.4 **Giving up some control is better than dying with all of it.** *Take the investor before the bank takes the shop.*
- 6.2.5 **Don't cover one hole by digging another.** *Taking a new loan to repay an old one is how small debts become a collapse.*

### Unit 7: Leading people through hard times

**Module 7.1: Honest leadership**
- 7.1.1 **Admit a big mistake fast and fix it.** *The new recipe failed. Bring back the old one and say sorry.*
- 7.1.2 **Pay your people first when money is short.** *Staff who go unpaid leave, and they talk.*
- 7.1.3 **Hard cuts done once are kinder than small cuts done every month.** *One clear restructuring, not twelve months of fear.*
- 7.1.4 **Peacetime and wartime need different leaders.** *In a crisis, decide fast and break routine. In calm times, build consensus.* (Horowitz.)

**Module 7.2: Owning the truth**
- 7.2.1 **Tell the full picture to the people you owe.** *Call the supplier before the cheque bounces, not after.*
- 7.2.2 **Never make public claims you can't prove.** *Accusing a rival on WhatsApp can cost you a defamation case and your name.*

### Unit 8: Rivals, partners and power

**Module 8.1: Who has power over your profit** (Porter's five forces)
- 8.1.1 **Profit is squeezed by rivals, buyers, suppliers, new entrants and substitutes.** *A chai stall near a station: many rivals, cheap substitutes, easy entry.*
- 8.1.2 **Alone, a small seller has no power. Together, small sellers do.** *Village milk farmers formed a co-operative and owned the plant (Amul).*
- 8.1.3 **If you can't walk away, you can't bargain.** *One big client is 70% of your sales, so they set your price.*
- 8.1.4 **Depending on a supplier who could become your rival is a risk. Plan to own the key piece.** *A food brand that only sells through one delivery app.*

**Module 8.2: When someone undercuts you**
- 8.2.1 **Don't join a price war you can't win. Win on cost or on a different value.** *A new shop sells 10% cheaper. Can your costs go 10% lower? If not, compete on service.*
- 8.2.2 **A low price only lasts if your costs are lower too.** *Nirma sold detergent at about a third of Surf's price because it kept costs very low.*
- 8.2.3 **A rich rival burning cash: survive, don't copy.** *When Jio made calls free, rivals' profits fell hard.*

**Module 8.3: Rivals as partners**
- 8.3.1 **Sometimes making peace with a rival saves your customers.** *Apple took Microsoft's money and kept Office on the Mac.*
- 8.3.2 **Pick the hungry partner over the famous one.** *A smaller supplier who needs you will try harder.*
- 8.3.3 **Launch with the partner who is ready, not the biggest one.** *A young chain that will stock you now beats a giant that "may consider" next year.*

### Unit 9: Strategy: making real choices

**Module 9.1: What strategy is** (Rumelt, Lafley and Martin)
- 9.1.1 **Strategy starts with a clear diagnosis of the biggest problem.** *"Sales are down" is a symptom. "Our area now buys online" is a diagnosis.*
- 9.1.2 **A goal is not a strategy.** *"₹1 crore this year" is a wish. How will you get there?*
- 9.1.3 **Strategy is choosing what NOT to do.** *No catering, no franchise, only our one café, done brilliantly.*
- 9.1.4 **Choose where to play and how to win, together.** *Small-town parents (where), English tuition in Tamil at half the city price (how).*
- 9.1.5 **Every action must support the same plan.** *Premium prices and dirty tables don't fit together.*

**Module 9.2: Position and focus**
- 9.2.1 **Don't copy a rival's costly extras. Give people a new reason to choose you.** *IndiGo dropped free meals and sold "on time".*
- 9.2.2 **Grow through your core, not away from it.** *A sweet shop adds gift boxes before it opens a restaurant.*
- 9.2.3 **Sell what others can run better, and go back to what you are best at.** *LEGO sold its theme parks.*
- 9.2.4 **Define yourself by what you're good at, not by what you used to sell.** *A printing press becomes a design-and-print studio.*
- 9.2.5 **When everything is a priority, cut hard.** *Apple went from dozens of models to four.*

### Unit 10: Moats: why some businesses can't be copied (7 Powers)

**Module 10.1: Cost moats**
- 10.1.1 **Scale: bigger volume lowers cost per piece, but only if you're much bigger than rivals.** *A wholesaler buying 100 tonnes gets a price a 1-tonne buyer never will.*
- 10.1.2 **Process power: a way of working so refined that others can't copy it quickly.** *Asian Paints' in-store tinting machines and dealer network.*

**Module 10.2: Customer moats**
- 10.2.1 **Brand: trust built over years lets you charge more.** *Families pay extra for a jeweller their grandparents trusted.*
- 10.2.2 **Switching costs: when leaving you is a hassle, customers stay.** *Your billing software holds all their GST records.*
- 10.2.3 **Network effects: each new user makes it better for everyone.** *A UPI app that every shop accepts.*

**Module 10.3: Special moats**
- 10.3.1 **Cornered resource: own something others can't get.** *The only licence, the best location, the one great product you won't share.*
- 10.3.2 **Counter-positioning: a new model the old leader can't copy without hurting itself.** *Zerodha's flat ₹20 fee was hard for brokers who charged a percentage to match.*
- 10.3.3 **One strong shared brand beats many weak ones.** *Gujarat's unions all selling as "Amul".*

### Unit 11: When the world changes (disruption)

**Module 11.1: Seeing it coming**
- 11.1.1 **Judge new things by how fast they improve, not by how good they are today.** *Phone cameras were poor in 2005 and killed the photo studio by 2015.*
- 11.1.2 **The thing that kills you often looks small and silly at first.** *"Who will buy clothes without touching them?"*
- 11.1.3 **Buy or back a small rival while it's cheap.** *The $50 million Netflix offer that Blockbuster turned down.*

**Module 11.2: Moving before you must**
- 11.2.1 **Give the new business its own team, money and freedom.** *Your online store shouldn't need the shop manager's approval.*
- 11.2.2 **Never slow down your new business to protect the old one.** *Don't price the app high "so the shop doesn't suffer".*
- 11.2.3 **Use what you already have as a weapon in the new game.** *Your shops become pickup and return points for online orders.*
- 11.2.4 **Leave a losing business before it drags you down.** *Intel left memory chips for microprocessors.*
- 11.2.5 **Let go of the famous part if it saves the company.** *Nokia sold phones and kept its networks business.*
- 11.2.6 **Join the winning platform when your own can't catch up.** *Sell on ONDC or Amazon rather than build your own marketplace.*

### Unit 12: Crises and big bets (advanced)

**Module 12.1: Crisis**
- 12.1.1 **Answer small warnings early and openly.** *One food-safety complaint: test publicly now.*
- 12.1.2 **Protect people first, then defend your product.** *Pull the batch first. Argue about the lab report later.*
- 12.1.3 **Fight through the proper channels, not in public.** *Appeal to the court or regulator, not through angry ads.*
- 12.1.4 **Come back only after you've proved it's fixed.** *Relaunch after the retests pass, then make the return a celebration.*

**Module 12.2: Big bets**
- 12.2.1 **Every big decision is a bet. Size it so a loss won't kill you.** *Put 20% of savings into the second shop, not 100%.*
- 12.2.2 **Don't buy a struggling rival while you're struggling.** *Two sick businesses make a sicker one.*
- 12.2.3 **Buying companies is not the same as growing. Each deal must fit and be digested.** *Byju's bought about 17 companies in 4 years, many at high prices, and struggled to merge them.*
- 12.2.4 **Go abroad or into a big new market to get better, not just bigger.** *Exporting to Europe forced Maruti's suppliers to raise quality.*
- 12.2.5 **When your model is working in a storm, change nothing.** *Rivals panic in a downturn. Hold your low costs and steady growth.*
- 12.2.6 **Double down early on what works.** *Order big while others hesitate.*
- 12.2.7 **Ethics is strategy: shortcuts with other people's money end in ruin.** *Every scam story ends the same way.*

122 lessons in 12 units and 31 modules. At 3–5 minutes each, that's about 6–10 hours of learning before Hot Seat practice is counted. If that's too long for launch, ship Units 1–4 and 8–9 first (about 70 lessons).

## 4. Sequencing: gentle and motivating early

1. **Open with wins, not theory.** Unit 1's first lesson is about money in the player's own pocket (1.1.1). The first Hot Seat after Units 1–2 should be cx 1–2 with a clear, satisfying answer: blockbuster (cx 1) or maruti-800. This matches the current rule that the first two Hot Seats have complexity ≤ 2.
2. **Learn 3–5 lessons, then practise.** Put a Hot Seat gate after every two modules, not after every unit. Duolingo's pull is from frequent unlocks.
3. **Practise only what's been taught.** Unlock each Hot Seat once the player has finished the lessons for its decisions (Section 2 gives the map). A cx-3 Hot Seat can draw on 3–4 units, and harshad-mehta (cx 4) on six, so keep those for later.
4. **Repeat lessons inside the story.** When a Hot Seat choice tests lesson 3.2.1, the "why" feedback names it: "Remember: discounts hide problems." This is cheap to author and does the job of spaced repetition.
5. **Hard truths come after the player is hooked.** Debt (Unit 6), firing people (Unit 7) and scandal (12.2.7) arrive after the player has a streak and some confidence.
6. **Order of units:** 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 (money and customers: what they need on Monday), then 5 → 8 (running and competing), then 9 → 10 (strategy and moats), then 6, 7, 11 and 12 interleaved (risk, leadership, disruption, crisis). Units 6 and 7 can start earlier as single lessons for players who report "struggling" in onboarding.
7. **Daily Calls as warm-ups.** Each Daily Call can practise one lesson from the player's current unit in one minute.
