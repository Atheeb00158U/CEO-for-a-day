## Candidates

### 1. Flipkart · 2010 · Pay when it arrives (India: yes · Area: distribution)
- Situation: Flipkart sold online, but few Indians used credit cards [1]. Couriers that collected cash took weeks to settle and charged high fees [2].
- The decision: Do you let customers pay cash at the door, even if it means running your own delivery?
- Real choice: Flipkart offered cash on delivery and started its own logistics arm as a pilot in December 2010 [2], because it did not want outside couriers holding its cash [1].
- Two realistic alternatives: accept cards only and grow slowly; offer COD through outside couriers and wait weeks for your money.
- What happened: Its eKart arm grew to serve over 800 cities [3].
- Lesson: If customers don't trust you yet, let them pay after they see the product.
- Risks: none. The exact COD share of orders was not found in an opened source.

### 2. Nirma · 1969 · A detergent for one-quarter the price (India: yes · Area: pricing)
- Situation: A government chemist began making detergent at home in 1969 [4]. The market leader, Surf, sold at Rs 13 [4].
- The decision: Do you price close to the big brand, or far below it for families who can't afford it?
- Real choice: He priced Nirma at Rs 3.50 per kg and sold it door to door by bicycle [4].
- Two realistic alternatives: price near Surf and hope quality wins; sell only through shops in one city.
- What happened: Nirma became India's largest-selling detergent within a decade [4]. Surf was badly hurt after Nirma's powder reached the mass market [5].
- Lesson: A huge market often waits below the big brand's price.
- Risks: none. Sources date the start to 1969 [4] but give 1975 for the mass launch [5].

### 3. Hindustan Lever · 1980s · Answer the cheap rival (India: yes · Area: product)
- Situation: Nirma's low-price powder took share from Hindustan Lever's premium Surf [5].
- The decision: Do you cut Surf's price, ignore Nirma, or launch a cheaper brand of your own?
- Real choice: HUL launched a low-cost brand, Wheel, to fight Nirma [6]. It kept Surf as its premium brand.
- Two realistic alternatives: cut Surf's price and hurt its margins; ignore Nirma as a "poor people's" brand.
- What happened: HUL's answer came only after a long delay [5]. The shock changed Unilever's strategy in poorer markets, including selling small sachets [5].
- Lesson: When a cheap rival attacks, fight with a second brand, and fight early.
- Risks: none. The exact Wheel launch year was not confirmed in an opened source.

### 4. CavinKare (Chik) · 1983 · Shampoo for small change (India: yes · Area: pricing)
- Situation: Shampoo came in bottles that many families could not afford [7]. The founder's family had already tried selling products in small sachets [7].
- The decision: Do you sell shampoo in bottles like the leaders, or in tiny packs at a tiny price?
- Real choice: Chik launched a 10 ml shampoo sachet in 1983 [8], sold at a very low price and pushed in rural markets [7].
- Two realistic alternatives: sell small bottles in cities; copy the leader's pricing and spend on ads.
- What happened: Within a couple of years Chik overtook Velvette and became South India's top shampoo [7].
- Lesson: Make the pack small enough for the customer's pocket.
- Risks: sources disagree on the launch price: 50 paise [8] or Re 1 [7].

### 5. Tata Tea · 2000 · The small fish buys the big fish (India: yes · Area: cash and funding)
- Situation: Tata Tea wanted to move from tea gardens to tea brands [9]. Its 1995 bid for Tetley failed for lack of money [9]. Tetley was the world's second-biggest tea company [9].
- The decision: Do you borrow heavily to buy a global brand bigger than you?
- Real choice: Tata Tea bought Tetley in 2000 for £271 million in a leveraged buyout (a deal funded largely by borrowing) [9][10].
- Two realistic alternatives: build its own brand abroad slowly; buy more plantations instead.
- What happened: Tetley became Tata's main global brand, and about two-thirds of Tata Tea's sales came from the Tetley Group [9].
- Lesson: Borrowing to buy a brand can work if its cash can repay the loan.
- Risks: none.

### 6. Titan · 1987 · Quartz only (India: yes · Area: product)
- Situation: A state-owned brand ruled Indian watches, selling mechanical watches that needed winding [12]. A 1985 policy change let makers choose their own mix [11].
- The decision: Do you make proven mechanical watches, or bet everything on quartz?
- Real choice: Titan, a Tata–TIDCO joint venture, made only quartz watches [11][12]. Its first range sold at Rs 350–900 [12].
- Two realistic alternatives: copy the leader's mechanical watches; sell a mix of both to stay safe.
- What happened: The old leader HMT went into slow decline [11]. Titan held over 60% of the organised watch market by 2012 [11].
- Lesson: When technology is changing, build for the new thing, not the old one.
- Risks: none.

### 7. Paper Boat (Hector Beverages) · 2013 · From energy drink to aam panna (India: yes · Area: product)
- Situation: Hector Beverages sold Tzinga, a cheap energy drink at Rs 25 against Red Bull's Rs 95 [13]. A co-founder's home-made aam panna gave the team a new idea [13].
- The decision: Do you keep pushing your energy drink, or bet on traditional Indian drinks?
- Real choice: It launched Paper Boat drinks in August 2013 in Delhi and Bengaluru, in pouches that cost less than cartons [13].
- Two realistic alternatives: spend more on Tzinga ads; launch Western-style juices like the big brands.
- What happened: By 2015 Paper Boat made about 90% of the company's sales [13].
- Lesson: Sell what customers already love but can't find in shops.
- Risks: none. One source date for the first drinks conflicts (2011 vs 2013) [13].

### 8. Zomato · 2022 · The 10-minute food promise (India: yes · Area: marketing)
- Situation: Zomato announced Zomato Instant, 10-minute food delivery, in March 2022 [14]. Critics said it could push riders to drive unsafely [14].
- The decision: Do you make a bold speed promise to grab headlines and customers?
- Real choice: Zomato piloted it in Gurugram, saying riders faced no penalties for late orders [14].
- Two realistic alternatives: test quietly with a few users first; promise a safer 20–30 minutes.
- What happened: Daily orders were too low to cover fixed costs [14]. By February 2023 the service was relaunched as Zomato Everyday, home-style meals from Rs 89 [15].
- Lesson: A big promise must also make money on each order.
- Risks: low. The founder is still active, so keep it about the service, not him.

### 9. Zerodha · 2010s · Say no to investor money (India: yes · Area: cash and funding)
- Situation: Zerodha, a low-cost online broker founded in 2010, became profitable in its second year [16].
- The decision: Do you raise venture money to grow faster, or grow only from your own profits?
- Real choice: It stayed bootstrapped and avoided big ad spending. The founder did not want to answer to investors at all hours [16].
- Two realistic alternatives: raise venture money and spend on ads; take a small loan to grow faster.
- What happened: In FY24 Zerodha earned a profit of about Rs 4,700 crore on Rs 8,320 crore revenue, still bootstrapped [17].
- Lesson: If customers pay you well, you may not need investors.
- Risks: low. Other sources give higher FY24 figures; use the cited ones.

### 10. Zoho · 2004 · Hire school-leavers, not graduates (India: yes · Area: hiring/people)
- Situation: Zoho, then called AdventNet, needed software talent. The founder believed people without degrees could do as well with training [18].
- The decision: Do you fight for graduates from top colleges, or train young people straight out of school?
- Real choice: In 2004 Zoho started Zoho University, a two-year course for Class 12 students. Students pay no fees and get a Rs 10,000 monthly stipend [18].
- Two realistic alternatives: pay more to win top-college graduates; outsource coding to agencies.
- What happened: Most students are hired by Zoho and are productive almost from the start [18]. Many come from low-income, often rural, families [18].
- Lesson: You can train talent instead of buying it.
- Risks: none. Graduate counts were not found in an opened source.

### 11. Cadbury India · 2003 · Worms before Diwali (India: yes · Area: crisis)
- Situation: In October 2003 worms were found in Cadbury products in Maharashtra [19]. The company first defended its factory standards and said it could not watch every shop [19]. The regulator moved to prosecute [19].
- The decision: Do you blame the shops, stay quiet, or spend money to fix trust?
- Real choice: Cadbury improved its packaging and hired a top film star whose ad described visiting the factory [20].
- Two realistic alternatives: keep blaming shop storage; cut prices to win buyers back.
- What happened: Sales fell after the news, then rebounded [20].
- Lesson: In a crisis, fix the problem and show the fix. Don't just defend yourself.
- Risks: low. Use the star's role, not his name.

### 12. Nestle India (Maggi) · 2015 · Pull everything off the shelves? (India: yes · Area: crisis)
- Situation: On 5 June 2015 the food regulator banned Maggi, citing lead and MSG above limits [21]. Maggi held about 75% of the noodles market [22].
- The decision: Do you fight in court only, or also pull and destroy all your stock?
- Real choice: Nestle recalled the noodles, destroyed stock worth Rs 320 crore, and challenged the ban in court [21].
- Two realistic alternatives: keep selling where states allowed it; fight in court before any recall.
- What happened: Maggi returned in November 2015 and regained just over 60% share by 2018 [22].
- Lesson: Protect trust first; you can win the argument later.
- Risks: borderline. A government consumer case was still going ahead as of the last report we opened [23]; avoid saying who was "right" about the tests.

### 13. Reliance Jio · 2016 · Give it all away free (India: yes · Area: pricing)
- Situation: Jio launched a new mobile network into a crowded market in September 2016 [24].
- The decision: Do you charge from day one, or give service free to win users fast?
- Real choice: Jio gave free data, calls and video from 5 September to 31 December 2016, then moved to paid plans [24].
- Two realistic alternatives: price a little below rivals from day one; give a short one-month free trial.
- What happened: The free offer ran about six months, and Jio reached 100 million users in 172 days [25].
- Lesson: "Free" can win users fast, but only if you can pay for it.
- Risks: medium. This is a giant-company move; tell players a small business could not afford this.

### 14. Hero Honda · 2010 · Go alone without your partner (India: yes · Area: cash and funding)
- Situation: Honda had opened its own Indian two-wheeler company, and the joint venture was strained [26]. Hero paid Honda royalties of about Rs 500 crore a year [27].
- The decision: Do you renegotiate with your partner, or buy them out and build your own technology?
- Real choice: On 16 December 2010, Hero agreed to buy Honda's 26% stake [26][27].
- Two realistic alternatives: stay a junior partner and keep paying royalties; sell your own stake.
- What happened: Hero had only 170 R&D staff at the split [27]. Its share fell from 45% to 39% by 2015-16 while Honda's grew [27]. It built an Rs 850 crore R&D centre and launched in-house scooters [27].
- Lesson: Independence costs money and time, so plan for both.
- Risks: borderline. Check current news on company leadership before publishing (not researched here).

### 15. Johnson & Johnson (Tylenol) · 1982 · Recall everything (India: no · Area: crisis)
- Situation: Seven people died after taking cyanide-laced Tylenol capsules from Chicago-area stores [28]. Tylenol held 35% of its market [28].
- The decision: Do you recall only in Chicago, or everywhere at huge cost?
- Real choice: J&J recalled 31 million bottles nationwide at a cost of $100 million [28].
- Two realistic alternatives: recall only in Chicago; issue warnings and wait for police results.
- What happened: Share fell to 7%, then returned to 35% by end-1983 after a tamper-proof relaunch [28].
- Lesson: Putting customer safety first can save the brand.
- Risks: involves deaths; keep the tone serious and simple.

### 16. Airbnb · 2008 · Sell cereal to stay alive (India: no · Area: cash and funding)
- Situation: Many early investors rejected Airbnb's idea of staying in strangers' homes [29].
- The decision: When nobody will fund you, do you quit, take a job, or hustle for cash?
- Real choice: The founders designed and sold election-themed cereal boxes at $40 each, raising about $30,000 [29].
- Two realistic alternatives: shut down and take jobs; borrow more on personal credit cards.
- What happened: The hustle impressed Y Combinator, which accepted them and invested $20,000 [29].
- Lesson: Showing grit can win your first backers.
- Risks: none.

### 17. Starbucks · 2008 · Close every store to retrain staff (India: no · Area: hiring/people)
- Situation: In January 2008 the chairman returned as CEO with a plan to fix the US customer experience, including new staff training [31].
- The decision: Do you train baristas quietly, or close every store and lose sales to show you are serious?
- Real choice: On 26 February 2008, Starbucks closed nearly 7,100 US stores for one evening to retrain baristas [30].
- Two realistic alternatives: train in small batches with no closures; just send training videos.
- What happened: Many called it a gimmick [32]. Starbucks also closed about 600 weak US stores, and Q4 FY08 profit fell to $5.4 million [33]. By fiscal 2010 it posted record results [34].
- Lesson: Your people are the product, so invest in their skills.
- Risks: the record 2010 results came from the whole turnaround, not just the training day. Say so in the reveal.

### 18. LEGO · 2004 · Fewer toys, back to bricks (India: no · Area: product)
- Situation: In 2003 LEGO was close to bankruptcy after moving away from its core brick business [35].
- The decision: Do you keep expanding into new businesses, sell the company, or shrink to the core?
- Real choice: The new CEO in 2004 controlled cash, sold the Legoland parks and video games, and cut the number of parts [35].
- Two realistic alternatives: sell the company; borrow more and keep all businesses.
- What happened: From 2009 revenue grew more than 20% a year [35].
- Lesson: When in trouble, cut back to what you do best.
- Risks: none.
