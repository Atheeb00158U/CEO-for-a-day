## Decision points

### D1 · 2016 · Discounts are buying sales at a loss
- Situation: Flipkart and Amazon have far more of the market [17]. Snapdeal's loss more than doubled in FY16 while staff costs tripled [3][4]. Reports say much cash went on discounts and ads [18].
- Options: (a) keep matching rivals' discounts to hold on to buyers; (b) cut discounts and ad spend, push sellers to pay more of any discount, and accept that sales will fall; (c) raise a large new round and keep spending until rivals blink.
- What it did: (b), mostly. It said it would chase revenue, not GMV [8], and its GMV fell the most of the big players in 2016, which one research firm links partly to lower discounts [19]. ⚠ The clearest dated source for "cut discounts from November 2015" is a Livemint report (14 Apr 2016) seen only second-hand; see Open questions.
- What happened next: Nine-month EBITDA improved about 40% [16][17], but the FY17 loss still grew to ₹4,647 crore, largely due to a ₹1,797 crore write-down [11]. Revenue fell to ₹903 crore [4].
- Tests: **5.2.5** (each customer must earn more margin than they cost). Supported.

### D2 · Feb 2017 · How long can we last?
- Situation: Reports put cash at US$208–238M, about 8–10 months of runway [16]. The valuation was falling: SoftBank had marked down its stake [3]. Talks to sell to Alibaba had collapsed [3]. ₹500 crore of money owed to Snapdeal had been written off in late 2015 [4].
- Options: (a) say "we are adequately funded" and keep the plan; (b) raise a new round at a lower price (reported talks with SoftBank [18]); (c) lay out profit, cash and debts side by side, cut the monthly burn hard and aim for profit within two years.
- What it did: Both (a) and (c): it said it was adequately funded [16] and that it would turn profitable in two years without new money unless it bought something [17], while cutting costs [3][16]. It did not close a new round at a lower price; Nexus and the founders put in ₹113 crore in May 2017 [1][4].
- What happened next: Cash fell from ₹356 crore (Mar 2017) to ₹222 crore (Sep 2017) [4].
- Tests: **9.1.1** (profit, cash and debts together), **1.2.5** (know your months of cash). Supported. **9.1.2** (profit up, cash down: stock and udhaar): only weakly. The ₹500 crore receivables write-off [4] is the one fact about money stuck with others; there is no source on Snapdeal's stock or seller payables. Suggest testing 9.1.2 through the receivables write-off in a scene, or leave 9.1.2 to the quiz.

### D3 · Feb–Aug 2017 · Cut costs: one deep cut, or slow trims?
- Situation: The company needed to stop burning cash. It had already trimmed delivery, hosting and fixed costs [16].
- Options: (a) one deep cut now: close or sell everything non-core and shrink the team once; (b) cut in rounds: about 600 jobs now [3], keep FreeCharge and Vulcan for now, then decide; (c) no layoffs: freeze hiring and cut salaries across the board.
- What it did: (b). Cuts came in several rounds: earlier cost cuts [16], about 600 jobs in Feb 2017 with the founders on zero salary [3], then, after the merger talks ended, reports of a much deeper cut, from about 1,200 staff down by up to about 1,000 [15][19]. FreeCharge and Vulcan were sold only in Jul 2017 and Jan 2018 [2][10].
- What happened next: Staff held town halls in April to steady morale [9]. Senior people left [3].
- Tests: **7.2.2** (one deep cut is kinder than many small ones). ⚠ The real history is the "slow trims" path, not the one the lesson favours. That can work: the real option becomes the "ok" one, with "better" = one deep cut, and the advisor can note that a second round came anyway. Atheeb should confirm this is acceptable. The second round's size (up to 1,000) is from media reports only [15]; the company never confirmed a number.

### D4 · May–Jul 2017 · Flipkart offers to buy you
- Situation: SoftBank, the largest investor, backed a sale to Flipkart [2]. Flipkart's offer rose from about US$700M to about US$850M [7] and reportedly US$900–950M [2], far below the US$6.5B peak [1]. Investors disagreed over price and a special payout to two early investors [1][7]. FreeCharge could be sold for ₹385 crore [2].
- Options: (a) accept Flipkart's offer and give up control; (b) stay alone: sell FreeCharge and Vulcan, shrink to a lean marketplace and try to pay your own way; (c) keep talking and hold out for a higher price, without cutting further.
- What it did: (b). On 31 Jul 2017 it ended all talks and announced Snapdeal 2.0 [2][14].
- What happened next: FreeCharge was sold for ₹385 crore [2] and Vulcan for ₹35 crore [10]. Snapdeal was cash flow positive for June 2018 [5]. The loss fell to ₹611 crore in FY18 and ₹186 crore in FY19 [6]. It later formed AceVector [1] and filed IPO papers twice [12][13]. It survived, but far smaller than Flipkart.
- Tests: **9.2.5** (give up control only if you can't survive alone). Supported: the company could survive alone and did. ⚠ Do not say why the founders or board chose this; reports about who blocked the deal are second-hand [7].

## What really happened
Snapdeal grew fast on investor money and hit a US$6.5B valuation in Feb 2016 [1]. Losses doubled that year [4], and it fell well behind Flipkart and Amazon [17]. It cut costs and jobs in rounds through 2017, and its founders took no salary [3][16]. In July 2017 it turned down Flipkart's offer, sold FreeCharge cheaply, and became a much smaller "Snapdeal 2.0" [2]. It was cash flow positive for one month in mid-2018 [5], and its yearly loss fell from ₹4,647 crore in FY17 to ₹186 crore in FY19 [6]. Real ending: **survivor**.

## Risks
- **Litigation:** Snapdeal and its founders have faced several cases: a 2017 Bengaluru magistrate summons over unpaid event sponsorship [21], and other reported cases over seller and vendor dues and fake products (seen in search results only, not opened; status unknown). None is about the 2016–17 decisions in this story, but check current status before publishing.
- **SEBI matters:** AceVector (Snapdeal's parent) has a confidential draft IPO filing pending with SEBI (Jul 2025) [13]. A story about Snapdeal's cash troubles could be read as comment on a company in an IPO process. Flag to Atheeb.
- **Living people:** both co-founders and the investors are alive and public. Keep to what the company did and announced; no claims about why anyone acted. Reports that "the founders refused to back the sale" are second-hand [7]; avoid.
- **Single-source or reported-only claims:** the 8–10 months runway is Factor Daily citing Mint, quoted by TechCrunch [16]; SoftBank's reported alternatives (a US$2.7B-valuation plan needing the founders to leave, or a US$300M six-month lifeline) appear only in Outlook Business [4] and must not go on the real path; the up-to-1,000 layoff figure is media reports [15].
- **Conflicting numbers:** FY16 loss is ₹3,316 crore [4][5], ₹3,315.5 crore [3], ₹3,340 crore [11] or ₹29.6 billion [17] (different filings). FreeCharge sale price is ₹385 crore [2][7][14] or ₹395 crore [4]. Use "about ₹3,300 crore" and "₹385 crore". FY18 loss ₹611 vs ₹613 crore [6]. Inc42 [3] puts a US$6.5–7B valuation on a "February 2017 round", which conflicts with Feb 2016 [1][2]; use Feb 2016.
- **Thin sources:** several key outlets (Livemint, ET, YourStory, Business Standard, The Ken) blocked our fetcher; the discount-cut story rests on secondary sources [8][18][19].
