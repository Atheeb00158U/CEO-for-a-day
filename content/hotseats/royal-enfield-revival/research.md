## Timeline
- 1990: Eicher buys 26% of Enfield India [9].
- 1993: Eicher takes a 60% majority stake [9].
- 1994: Enfield India is renamed Royal Enfield Motors [9]; sources call this the year Eicher acquired it [10][12]. (Other dates: merger 1994 [6]; 1995 in [13]; 1996 amalgamation [9].)
- 1999: Siddhartha Lal, the chairman's son and a trained automotive engineer, joins Eicher, working in tractor marketing [2][5].
- 2000: Royal Enfield sells under 2,000 bikes a month, about a third of its capacity [3][11]. It has large, piled-up losses and the board is weighing closure or sale [1][2][11].
- 2000: Lal, 26, becomes Royal Enfield's CEO and works from Chennai [5]; the board gives him a chance [2][3].
- 2000–2002: Owners complain of oil leaks, engine seizure and electrical faults [1][2][12]. Bikes reach dealers damaged; packing changes from straw to Styrofoam [2]. A new client service team answers dealers [1].
- 2002: The Jaipur plant stops making motorcycles [6].
- 2002 (Auto Expo): The Thunderbird cruiser is unveiled, with a 5-speed gearbox and the shifter moved to the left [7][8]; the company aims for a new model about every six months [8].
- Early 2000s: The first company store opens in Adyar, Chennai, selling Thunderbird and Electra [2].
- 2004 (Jan): Lal becomes Eicher's group COO [5]. Royal Enfield's decline has stopped at about 25,000 bikes a year [1][2]. (Some sources date 25,000 to 2005 [3][12].)
- 2005: Eicher decides to sell 13 of its 15 businesses, with a six-month plan made with BCG [2][5]; tractors and engines go to TAFE [9]. It keeps only motorcycles and trucks [3][10].
- 2006 (May): Lal becomes CEO and MD of Eicher Motors [5].
- 2008: Eicher puts its truck business into VECV, a joint venture with Volvo [5][9][10].
- 2009: The new Unit Construction Engine (UCE) launches on the Classic [4][10]; the Classic 350/500 enter production [6].
- 2010: All models move to the UCE [10][12]; sales reach about 50,000, the plant's limit; waits of six months or more [3][13].
- 2012: Plan for 100,000 bikes; 175 sales and service points in India; exports to 30+ countries [10]. Q3 2012 sales 30,046, up 49.7% [4].
- 2013: A second factory opens at Oragadam, Tamil Nadu [9]; capacity planned at 150,000 a year [10].

## Decision points

### D1 · 2000 · Sell it, shut it, or fix it?
- Situation: Sales are under 2,000 bikes a month, a third of capacity [3][11]. Losses are large [1][11]. The board is weighing closure or sale [1][2][11]. The bike is seen as one for an older relative [2].
- Options: (a) sell the bike unit to a buyer and put the money into trucks and tractors; (b) shut it down and stop the losses; (c) keep it and try to fix it, with a young new CEO.
- What they did: (c). The board let Lal, 26, try [2][3][5].
- What happened next: Lal first thought a new design would stop the losses, then found deeper problems: quality, dealers, working capital, labour, and no engineering or marketing leaders [2]. By 2004 the decline stopped at about 25,000 bikes a year [1][2].
- Tests: **10.1.1** (name the real problem). Good fit: the sign is "falling sales and losses"; the real problem is bikes that break and reach dealers damaged, not a lack of new designs [2]. A follow-up option inside this call or D2 can contrast "a new model" with "fix the old bike first".

### D2 · 2000–2002 · Fix the bike, or launch new ones?
- Situation: Owners report oil leaks, seized engines and electrical faults [1][2][12]. Bikes reach dealers scratched and torn [2]. Dealers say the company doesn't respond [1].
- Options: (a) launch a flashy new model fast to bring buyers back; (b) cut the price to clear stock and win volume; (c) fix the basics first: packing, leaks, the gear shift, dealer service.
- What they did: Mainly (c), alongside new models. Straw packing gave way to Styrofoam [2]; a client service team for dealers [1]; oil leak fixed and gear shift moved [3]; die-cast parts and CNC machining replaced manual work [4]. New models also came about every six months [8]. Lal warned that discounting harms the brand [2].
- What happened next: Decline halted by 2004 at about 25,000 a year [1][2]; dealer and buyer trust improved [2].
- Tests: **10.1.4** (every action supports one plan). Supported for "no discounting" and "fix quality"; less clean because the company also launched new models at the same time [8]. Make option (b) the costly one (it breaks the premium plan) and (a) the risky one.

### D3 · 2002 · Who to build for, and how to win them
- Situation: Young buyers prefer cheaper, fuel-efficient Japanese-style bikes and later the Pulsar [12][14]. Royal Enfield's loyal riders love the old Bullet's look and thump [1][2].
- Options: (a) build a light, cheap commuter to fight the big makers on mileage and price; (b) keep making only the old Bullet for the loyal riders; (c) keep the look and thump, but make it easier and more modern for a wider, younger crowd: a cruiser, left-side gears, nicer stores.
- What they did: (c). The Thunderbird cruiser at Auto Expo 2002, to soften the image beyond tough men [8]; shifter moved to the left [7][8]; expert help to keep the thump [2]; a first store in Adyar, Chennai, that made the brand look cool to young riders [2]. Later framed as a leisure bike bought for practical use, with purchase barriers removed [10].
- What happened next: Newer models drew younger city riders [4]; rides like the Himalayan Odyssey built a community [4][12].
- Tests: **10.1.3** (where to play and how to win, together). Option (a) is our estimate; no source says Eicher considered a commuter bike.

### D4 · 2004–05 · Fifteen businesses or two?
- Situation: Lal is now group COO with a free hand [2][5]. Eicher runs 15 businesses, including garments, shoes and food, many of them weak [2]. Tractors are a large, well-known part of Eicher [9].
- Options: (a) keep all 15 and fix them one by one; (b) keep tractors, trucks and bikes, and sell the small ones; (c) sell 13 businesses, even tractors, and keep only trucks and motorcycles.
- What they did: (c). The 2005 decision to sell 13 of 15, with a six-month BCG plan [2][5]; tractors and engines to TAFE [9][10]; some senior managers left with the tractor business [2].
- What happened next: The truck business went into a Volvo joint venture in 2008 [5][9]; money and attention went to Royal Enfield [12].
- Tests: **10.1.2** (strategy is choosing what not to do). Strong fit; selling the famous tractor business is the "harsh but right" call.

### D5 · 2008–2010 · Grow now, or rebuild the engine first?
- Situation: Sales are rising but the old engine is still unreliable [13]. A new engine with fewer parts is ready [12]; the plant is small [13].
- Options: (a) push the old bikes harder to grow volume now; (b) move every model to the new engine, even if the change year is hard and stock runs short; (c) build a big new factory first, then change the engine.
- What they did: (b). UCE on the Classic in 2009 [4]; all models moved in 2010, "the year of transition" [10][12].
- What happened next: Sales hit about 50,000, the plant's limit, with six-month waits [3][13]; the company planned 100,000 for 2012 and a new plant at Oragadam [10], which opened in 2013 [9].
- Tests: **10.1.4** again (quality before growth), with **10.3.1** (grow big once the small version works) as a bonus code. Optional: use it if a fifth call is needed.

## What really happened
In 2000 Royal Enfield sold under 2,000 bikes a month and Eicher's board was weighing closure [3][11]. A 26-year-old engineer, Siddhartha Lal, got the chance to fix it [2][5]. He fixed packing, leaks and dealer service, moved the gear shift, and launched the Thunderbird cruiser to widen the buyers [1][2][8]. By 2004 the fall had stopped at about 25,000 bikes a year [1][2]. In 2005 Eicher sold 13 of its 15 businesses, tractors included, to focus on motorcycles and trucks [2][9]. A new engine reached every model by 2010, sales hit the plant's limit of about 50,000 with long waits, and a second factory opened in 2013 [3][9][10]. Ending tier: legend.

## Risks
- Living people: Siddhartha Lal is living and now Eicher's executive chairman [5]. The story is about his business calls only. Keep out his family, his pay vote (2021) and any political or private items listed in [5].
- Family business: the board gave the chairman's son the job [3]. Write it plainly, without suggesting favouritism or merit beyond the sources.
- Litigation: none found about the 2000–2010 events. Not searched in depth; Atheeb may want a quick check before publishing.
- Thin and friendly sources: almost all accounts are admiring profiles and the company's own words [1][2][4][10]. No loss figure for 2000 was found in any opened source.
- Conflicting facts: Eicher's takeover is dated 1990 (26%), 1993 (60%), 1994, 1995 and 1996 [6][9][10][12][13]; we say "Eicher had owned it for a few years". 25,000 bikes is dated to 2004 [1][2] and 2005 [3][12]; we say "by about 2004". The exits are dated 2004 [3], 2005 [2][5] and 2006 [13]; we use 2005 (Eicher's own page [9] and Lal's interview [2]). The Thunderbird's first year is 2000 in Wikipedia's text [7] but 2002 in its infobox and in [8][15]; we use 2002. Classic/UCE: 2009 [4][6] vs "2010" in [13]; we use 2009 for launch, 2010 for all models.
- Curriculum fit: 10.1.4 is only partly clean in D2 because new models came at the same time as the fixes [8]. 10.1.1, 10.1.2 and 10.1.3 are well supported.
- Logos: Royal Enfield's badge is on every tank; crop or blur in all images.
