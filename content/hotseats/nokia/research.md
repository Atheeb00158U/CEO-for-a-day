## Timeline
- 2006: Nokia's factory at Sriperumbudur, near Chennai, starts production [15].
- 2007-Q4: Nokia sells 40% of all mobile phones in the world; full-year 2007 share is 37.8% on 435 million phones [2].
- 2007: Nokia holds almost 50% of the world smartphone market [6].
- 2008-06: Nokia offers €264 million for the 52% of Symbian it does not own and plans to make Symbian open source [3].
- 2010-02: Nokia and Intel merge their Linux software into one platform called MeeGo [4].
- 2010-09: Nokia's board replaces its long-time CEO with a Microsoft executive, as Nokia's handset share slips from 37.7% (2009) to 36.1% (Q2 2010) [1].
- 2010-Q4: Nokia's smartphone share is down to 30.8%, while Apple has 16% [6].
- 2011-02: Nokia makes Windows Phone its main smartphone platform; its share price falls 9.5% that morning [5].
- 2011-06: Nokia launches dual-SIM phones in India [9]; it also shows the N9, its only MeeGo phone [7].
- 2011-10: Nokia launches the Lumia 800, its first Windows Phone, at €420 [8].
- 2012-03: In urban India, Samsung passes Nokia in retail revenue for the first time (34.2% vs 33.8%) [9].
- 2012-Q4: Samsung has 22.7% of world phone sales, Nokia 18%, Apple 9.2%; Symbian falls to 1.2% of smartphone sales [11].
- 2013-07: Nokia agrees to buy Siemens' half of Nokia Siemens Networks for €1.7 billion [12].
- 2013-09: Nokia agrees to sell its phone business to Microsoft for €5.44 billion [13].
- 2014-04: The sale closes; Nokia names its networks chief as CEO and keeps Networks, HERE maps and patents [14].
- 2014-11: Chennai factory stops making phones after Microsoft ends its contract [15].

## Decision points
### D1 · 2008 · Own the smartphone software?
- Situation: Symbian was the top smartphone system with 57.1% share in Q1 2008, and Nokia owned about 48% of it [3]. Google's Android was arriving as a new open-source rival [3].
- Options a CEO realistically had (3, incl. what the company did): (a) keep sharing Symbian with other phone makers as before; (b) buy all of Symbian and give it away free as open source; (c) start moving to a new, modern software base.
- What the company did: Nokia paid about €264 million for the rest of Symbian and planned to make the code open source [3].
- What happened next: By Q4 2010 Nokia's smartphone share had dropped to 30.8% [6].

### D2 · 2010 · Build a new platform with a partner
- Situation: Nokia had its own Linux system (Maemo) and Intel had Moblin; both faced pressure from Android [4].
- Options a CEO realistically had (3, incl. what the company did): (a) put all effort into fixing Symbian; (b) join with Intel to build MeeGo for phones, tablets and netbooks; (c) adopt Android.
- What the company did: Nokia and Intel merged their systems into MeeGo, hosted by the Linux Foundation [4].
- What happened next: The board changed CEO in September 2010 after weak results and lost smartphone ground [1]. Only one MeeGo phone, the N9, was ever launched [7].

### D3 · 2011 · Choose the smartphone platform
- Situation: Nokia's smartphone share had fallen from almost 50% in 2007 to 30.8% in Q4 2010 [6].
- Options a CEO realistically had (3, incl. what the company did): (a) keep building on Symbian and MeeGo; (b) adopt Android like Samsung, LG and HTC [6]; (c) partner with Microsoft on Windows Phone.
- What the company did: Nokia made Windows Phone its main platform, demoted Symbian, and turned MeeGo into a long-term project [5]. Nokia said Android would make it a commodity maker and give Google too much services revenue [6].
- What happened next: Windows Phone held only 3.4% of the smartphone market at the time [6]. The first Lumia arrived in October 2011 [8], and by Q4 2012 Symbian was down to 1.2% of smartphone sales [11].

### D4 · 2011–2012 · Defend India and cheap phones
- Situation: Nokia once held about 70% of India's phone market, but had slid for nine quarters [9]. About half of Indian phone buyers wanted dual-SIM phones [9].
- Options a CEO realistically had (3, incl. what the company did): (a) focus on expensive smartphones only; (b) fight local brands with cheap dual-SIM and Asha feature phones; (c) cut prices on Lumia smartphones for India.
- What the company did: Nokia launched dual-SIM phones in June 2011, sold Asha phones at Rs 3,000–8,000, and gave retailers more margin [9].
- What happened next: Nokia's India shipment share fell from 31% in 2011 to 22.2% in H1 2012, though it stayed number one [10]. In smartphones, Samsung led India with about 43% to Nokia's 29% in March 2012 [9].

### D5 · 2013 · Double down on networks
- Situation: Nokia Siemens Networks was Nokia's most profitable unit, with €822 million adjusted operating profit in 2012, and Siemens wanted out [12].
- Options a CEO realistically had (3, incl. what the company did): (a) list NSN on the stock market; (b) sell Nokia's half too; (c) buy Siemens' half.
- What the company did: Nokia bought Siemens' 50% for €1.7 billion [12].
- What happened next: Nokia's shares rose more than 7% that day, but its net cash was expected to drop to €2.0–2.5 billion [12].

### D6 · 2013 · Sell the phone business
- Situation: The phone business made €14.9 billion revenue in 2012 but lost over €1 billion [13].
- Options a CEO realistically had (3, incl. what the company did): (a) keep fighting with Lumia; (b) switch to Android phones; (c) sell phones and focus on networks, maps and patents.
- What the company did: Nokia sold the phone business to Microsoft for €5.44 billion, including €1.65 billion for a patent licence, and about 32,000 staff moved [13].
- What happened next: Nokia rebuilt around Networks, HERE maps and patents, with its networks chief as CEO [14]. The Chennai plant, stuck in a tax freeze, was left out and stopped production [15].

## What really happened
Nokia was the world's biggest phone maker, with 40% of sales at the end of 2007 [2]. Its smartphone share fell fast after 2007, and in 2011 it bet on Microsoft's Windows Phone instead of Android [6]. Samsung overtook it worldwide by Q4 2012 [11]. In 2013 Nokia bought full control of its profitable networks business [12] and sold its phone business to Microsoft [13]. From 2014 it became mainly a networks company [14].

## People (by role)
- CEO 2006–2010: Olli-Pekka Kallasvuo, long-time Nokia insider [1].
- CEO 2010–2013: Stephen Elop, formerly head of Microsoft's Office division; moved with the phone business [1][13].
- Chairman and interim CEO 2013–2014: Risto Siilasmaa [13].
- CEO from 2014: Rajeev Suri, previously head of Nokia Siemens Networks [14].

## Risks
- Living people central to the story: all four CEOs are living; describe by role, avoid blame on individuals.
- Chennai tax dispute: Indian tax claims froze the plant [15]; current legal status not verified.
- Thin sources: BBC, Reuters and Nokia's own site blocked our fetcher, so most sources are trade press (Light Reading, PCWorld, InfoWorld).
- Conflicting India numbers: "about 70% peak" [9] vs 31% in 2011 [10] use different measures (retail vs shipments).
- Footage: Nokia logos and phone designs are trademarks even in CC0 clips.
