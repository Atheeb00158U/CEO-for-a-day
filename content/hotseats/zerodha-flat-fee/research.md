## Decision points

### D1 · 2010 · How to charge
- Situation: Indian brokers charged a percentage of each trade [2]. A new broker with seven people [4] and ₹2 crore [1] needs active traders to switch.
- Options: (a) charge a percentage like everyone else, a little lower; (b) a flat ₹20 per order, however big the trade; (c) a monthly fee for unlimited trades.
- What it did: (b). A flat ₹20 per executed order regardless of size [4].
- What happened next: About 7,000 clients in 15 months [4]; 30,000+ by 2012–13 [4]; profitable in year two [7].
- Tests: **11.1.4** (pick a model the leader can't copy without hurting itself). Supported in spirit: big brokers' income came from percentage fees [2]. ⚠ We have no opened source showing incumbents refused to copy, or when they did (ICICI Direct's flat-₹20 "Neo" plan in Dec 2020 was seen only in a search snippet). **3.1.1** (price from the buyer's value): weak. The founder's stated reason, seen in a search snippet only, was that a trade costs the same to run whatever its size, which is cost-based. Use 3.1.1 as a contrast (value to an active trader is huge savings), or leave it to the quiz.

### D2 · 2012–2014 · Getting customers: ads or free lessons?
- Situation: Clients were growing without paid ads [4]. Rivals were large, known brands. Most Indians did not invest at all (about a million active investors [6]).
- Options: (a) spend on TV and print ads; (b) give away free lessons on investing and answer questions in public; (c) build a sales team and franchise network, as older brokers did.
- What it did: (b), with no paid advertising [4][8][9]. Varsity launched free in Nov 2014 [5]. Its philosophy page says it does not advertise and gives free education with no forced sign-up [9].
- What happened next: 1.9 lakh+ clients by Dec 2016 [7]; 15% of retail turnover by 2019 "without spending any money on advertising" [8].
- Tests: **5.2.2** (happy customers and free teaching can beat paid ads) supported. **4.2.1** (give first) fits Varsity being free [5][9]. ⚠ Varsity came four years after launch; early growth (2010–13) came before it [4][5]. Set this call in 2014. We have no source that measures how many clients came via Varsity.

### D3 · 2012 · Let happy customers bring friends
- Situation: 30,000+ clients, no ads [4].
- Options: (a) pay a one-time cash bonus for each new account; (b) share part of the brokerage with clients who refer friends, for as long as the friend trades; (c) no reward: trust word of mouth alone.
- What it did: (b). By 2012–13, 4,000+ clients were referring others through the Zerodha Associate Program [4]; it shared brokerage with referrers until Apr 2018 [10]. (The later 10% scheme ran 2020–2024 and resumed 2026 [10][12].)
- What happened next: About 30% of accounts came through referral links [12]; word of mouth is named as the main driver of growth [9].
- Tests: **4.2.2** (people follow what others like them do). Fits: friends and colleagues bringing each other in [12]. ⚠ The 30% figure covers the whole history up to 2024, not 2012 [12]; the exact percentage shared before 2018 is not confirmed. Regulators twice questioned brokerage-sharing (2018 [10], 2024 [11]): option (b) carries a real rule risk, which an outcome can mention.

### D4 · 2015–2016 · Investors offer money: raise, or grow from profits?
- Situation: Profitable since year two [7]; revenue about ₹120 crore and pre-tax profit ₹65–66 crore in FY16 [7]; growing about 100% a year [7].
- Options: (a) take venture money to grow faster and advertise; (b) grow only from your own profits and stay independent; (c) sell a stake to a big bank in return for its branches and customers.
- What it did: (b). It declined outside funding [7]; when it made equity delivery free in Dec 2015 it said this was not funded by VC or PE money [6]. ET named it Startup of the Year (Bootstrap) in 2016 [2].
- What happened next: Largest retail broker by active clients in 2019 [1], still bootstrapped [3][9].
- Tests: **9.2.2** (grow on customers' money). Supported: fees from customers funded growth. ⚠ No opened source names a specific investor offer; "investors offer money" must stay general ("investors come calling") unless a source is found. Note the ₹2 crore seed included money from a friend [1], so "no outside money at all" is not accurate.

### Optional D5 · Nov 2015 · Make investing free?
- Situation: A flat ₹20 was already cheap [4]; India had only about a million active investors [6].
- Options: (a) keep ₹20 on everything; (b) charge nothing for buying shares to hold, and keep ₹20 on trading; (c) cut all fees in half.
- What it did: (b) from 1 Dec 2015 [6]; about 90,000 users came [7].
- Fits 11.1.4 and 3.1.1 again. Use only if a fifth call is wanted (curriculum lists four).

## What really happened
Zerodha went live on 15 Aug 2010 with a flat ₹20 a trade [3][4]. It grew without paid ads, through word of mouth and client referrals [4][9], and from 2014 through free Varsity lessons [5]. It turned profitable in year two and declined outside funding [7]. In Dec 2015 it made investing in shares free [6]. By Dec 2016 it had 1.9 lakh+ clients and ₹65–66 crore pre-tax profit [7], and in Jan 2019 it became India's largest retail broker by active clients [1]. Real ending: **legend**.

## Risks
- **Living people:** all four people above are alive and very public. Keep to what Zerodha did and announced. Its founders' reasons appear in the company's own blog [4][6]; the story may describe what the company said, but must not quote anyone or claim motives. Avoid the founders' personal lives entirely.
- **SEBI and exchange matters:** Zerodha is a regulated broker. Its referral payouts were stopped by a regulatory gap in 2018 [10] and by an NSE circular in 2024 [11]; they resumed in 2026 [12]. No SEBI order against Zerodha was found in our search. Any line on referrals should not imply the 2010s scheme broke rules.
- **Promoting trading:** a game for new entrepreneurs that celebrates a broker built on intraday and F&O fees could read as encouraging trading. SEBI has published studies on retail F&O losses (not opened for this brief). Suggest the story stays on pricing and growth, not on trading itself.
- **Company's own account:** sources [3][4][5][6][8][9][10][11][12] are Zerodha's or its founder's. Independent sources here are Inc42 [7] and Wikipedia [1][2].
- **Conflicting numbers:** founding team 4 [7], 6 [1] or 7 [4]; clients "30,000+" by 2012–13 [4] vs "three years to reach 10,000" [7]; Rainmatter 2014 [1] vs 2015 [2][7]. Use words like "a handful of people" and "tens of thousands of clients".
- **"Flat ₹20":** at launch it was ₹20 or 0.01%, whichever was lower [4]; so ₹20 was the most a trade would cost. Write it as "never more than ₹20 a trade" if exactness matters.
