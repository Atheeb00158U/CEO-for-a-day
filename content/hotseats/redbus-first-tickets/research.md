## Timeline
- October 2005: A 24-year-old engineer at Texas Instruments in Bengaluru tries to get home for Diwali (562 km). Travel agents have no seats, trains are full, flights cost too much; he spends Diwali alone [1][2][3].
- Late 2005: He sees there is no single list of bus operators, routes or free seats, as there is for trains and planes [1][4]. Agents only know their own operators' seats [5].
- Late 2005: First plan: a website listing private bus routes from Bengaluru [3]. Flatmates collect data at weekends on 300+ routes [3]. He finds about 50 buses leave for Hyderabad each evening, not the 5 he thought [3].
- About two months in: They switch from an information site to booking software for operators [3]. The team of seven splits: three build, four collect data [3].
- Early 2006: He pitches the software to 20 bus operators; none commits [3]. Operators doubt online booking [4] and aren't interested [5]. Most flatmates drop out; three stay [3].
- February 2006: Their plan wins a TiE Bangalore business plan contest; the prize is mentoring [3]. A mentor tells them to stop selling software and sell bus seats like a travel agent, to prove they can sell [2][3][6].
- Mid-2006: Two operators agree to give them a few seats [1][3]. Setting up card payments with a bank takes about a month [3].
- August 2006: The three founders quit their jobs and pool a small sum (₹30,000 [3]; ₹5 lakh in other sources [2][5][8]). Their flat is the office [3]. redBus.in goes live with two operators and two routes [1][3].
- 22 August 2006: First ticket sold, Bengaluru to Tirupati [5].
- First month: 10 seats sold [3]. Founders hand out cards outside offices and pamphlets at bus stops [1][4]. Early seats are sold by phone and the founders deliver the tickets themselves [1].
- October 2006: About ₹7,000 a month from ticket sales and ₹5,000 from ads [3].
- 2007: First venture round from Seedfund [3][8].
- January 2007: Bengaluru pays for itself; Hyderabad office opens [3].
- April 2007: Chennai [3]. August 2007: Mumbai and Delhi [3].
- December 2007: 15,000 tickets sold in the month [3]. 2007–08 turnover: about ₹5 crore [3].
- Later: operators start paying a share of seats sold, and redBus sells them its seat software (BOSS) [1][2][6]. 2013: sold to Ibibo [5][7][8].

## Decision points

### D1 · Late 2005 · What to build first
- Situation: You missed Diwali because no one could tell you which bus had a free seat [1][5]. Travellers can't see seats; operators sell through walk-ins and agents [2].
- Options: (a) a free website that lists bus routes and times; (b) booking software that bus operators buy to manage their seats; (c) a site where travellers book a seat, with you selling the seats.
- What they did: (a) first [3], then within about two months (b) [3]; Wharton and Deccan Chronicle describe the operator software as the first real product [1][5].
- What happened next: no operator bought it (D3).
- Tests: **2.1.1** (sell what people already struggle with). Supported, and nicely: the person with the pain was the traveller, but they first built for operators, who felt no pain [5]. The real call here is the risky one; (c) is the lesson's answer, which they reached only after D3.

### D2 · Late 2005 – early 2006 · How to learn
- Situation: You believe 5 buses an evening go to Hyderabad. You need to know how the business really works [3].
- Options: (a) go and count: visit bus offices and agents and write down what buses, seats and prices really exist; (b) show operators your software and ask if they'd use it; (c) run an online survey asking travellers if they would book buses online.
- What they did: both (a) and (b). Flatmates spent weekends collecting real data on 300+ routes and found 50 buses, not 5 [3]. The founder also pitched software to 20 operators [3]. He later wondered whether he had spoken to enough agents [2] (paraphrase; no quote).
- What happened next: the data was right; the pitches failed [3]. The mentor later told them to ask what customers need instead of pitching the same product [6].
- Tests: **2.1.3** (what they did, not what they'd do): the weekend count is the real-path example. **2.1.2** (ask about their life, not your idea): the 20 pitches are the counter-example. Supported. Suggest the real option be (a), since it's sourced and was what found the 50 buses; (c) is invented for balance.

### D3 · Early 2006 · Operators are polite, but no one signs
- Situation: 20 operators heard the pitch; none committed [3]. Most of your team has quit [3]. Some operators may have said nice things, but no source says that; write it as "no one said yes".
- Options: (a) keep improving the software until operators sign; (b) give the software away free to get the first operators; (c) stop selling software: buy a few seats from operators and sell them to travellers yourself, like an agent, to prove you can sell.
- What they did: (c), on mentors' advice after the February 2006 TiE contest [2][3][6].
- What happened next: two operators gave them seats [1][3]; once redBus showed it could sell seats, operators agreed to pay a share of each seat sold [6]; the software came back later as BOSS [1][2].
- Tests: **2.1.4** (praise is not proof; money or a firm promise is). Supported: the proof was seats sold, not interest. Caution: the curriculum's framing ("operators love the idea: build anyway, or wait until some promise you seats") overstates it; sources say operators were doubtful or uninterested [4][5], not enthusiastic. Recast as "operators listen, nod, and don't sign".

### D4 · Mid-2006 · Where to start
- Situation: You have two operators willing to give you a few seats [1][3], three people, and very little money [3].
- Options: (a) launch in Bengaluru only, with the few seats you have, and make it work there first; (b) launch in five big cities at once so the site looks big; (c) wait until you have 50 operators before going live.
- What they did: (a). Two operators, two routes, about 10 seats a day [1] (two seats a weekday per [3]); first ticket 22 Aug 2006, Bengaluru–Tirupati [5]. Bengaluru paid for itself by January 2007 before they opened Hyderabad (Jan), Chennai (Apr), Mumbai and Delhi (Aug 2007) [3].
- What happened next: 15,000 tickets in December 2007 [3].
- Tests: **2.2.1** (choose who you serve first). Supported. The curriculum says "one busy route"; the record says one city with two routes. Write "one city, a couple of routes".

### D5 · Late 2006 · How to get the first buyers (optional)
- Situation: The site is live but few people know it, and many don't trust paying online [5].
- Options: (a) spend on online ads to bring people to the website; (b) hand out cards outside offices, take bookings by phone, and deliver tickets and collect cash at the door; (c) sell only through travel agents.
- What they did: (b) [1][4][6]; later also agents and cyber cafes [3]; the mentor told them the internet was only one channel [6].
- What happened next: 3 to 50 people in nine months [4]; Bengaluru self-sustaining by January 2007 [3].
- Tests: none of the five assigned codes directly; fits quiz codes 2.2.4 (people buy a result: a seat home, not a website) and 2.2.2. Use only if a fifth call is needed.

## What really happened
An engineer missed Diwali in 2005 because no one could find him a bus seat [1]. With two friends he first built a route list, then software for bus operators, which no one bought [3]. A mentor told them to sell seats like an agent instead [2][3]. In August 2006 they quit their jobs and went live in Bengaluru with two operators and a handful of seats [1][3]; the first ticket went to Tirupati [5]. They delivered tickets by hand [1]. Bengaluru paid for itself by January 2007, then they opened four more cities in 2007 [3]. By December 2007 they sold 15,000 tickets a month [3]. In 2013 the company was sold to Ibibo [5][8].

## Risks
- **Living people:** all four key people are alive. Tell it by role ("you", "your two friends", "a mentor"). No quotes. Skip the founder's later personal reasons for selling [7]; that's private and outside the story.
- **Founder's own telling:** every early fact comes from the founders or mentor via interviews. No independent record. Fine for a founder story, but keep the real path to what two or more sources agree on where possible.
- **Conflicts:**
  - Home town: Hyderabad [1][2][3] vs Nizamabad [5] (Nizamabad is in the same state; he may have been going via Hyderabad). Use "home".
  - Starting money: ₹30,000 pooled [3] vs ₹5 lakh [2][5][8]. Use "their savings".
  - Launch seats: two seats a weekday [3] vs 10 seats a day on two routes [1] vs two seats [5]. Use "a handful of seats".
  - Order of first products: info site then software [3]; open-source platform [2]; not-for-profit tool [5]. All agree the first product was for operators, and it failed.
  - Sale price 2013: ₹680 crore [5] vs about $125 million [7]. Not needed in the story.
- **Litigation:** no case touches 2005–07. Recent consumer-court cases against redBus (refunds, route changes), a 2017 Delhi transport department case over illegal routes, and a December 2025 NCLT-approved merger of redBus India into MakeMyTrip India appeared in search results (not opened). Not part of the story, but flag to Atheeb that the company is in ongoing consumer litigation.
