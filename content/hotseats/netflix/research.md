## Timeline
- 2000: The founders offer to sell Netflix to Blockbuster for $50 million, and Blockbuster says no [1].
- 2002-03: Netflix files to go public with over 500,000 subscribers, a $19.95 monthly plan and no late fees [2].
- 2004: Blockbuster launches its own online DVD subscription service [1].
- 2006: Blockbuster launches Total Access, letting online members return DVDs in stores [1].
- 2006-12: Netflix reaches 6 million subscribers [1].
- 2007-01: Netflix launches "Watch Now" streaming on PCs at no extra charge, with about 1,000 titles [3].
- 2010: Blockbuster files for bankruptcy, and Netflix reaches 20 million subscribers [1].
- 2010-11: Netflix adds a $7.99 streaming-only plan and raises DVD plan prices [4].
- 2011-03: Netflix wins rights to House of Cards with a two-season order [10].
- 2011-07: Netflix stock peaks near $304, a market value over $16 billion [5].
- 2011-07: Netflix splits DVD and streaming into separate plans; the combined price rises from about $10 to $16 [6].
- 2011-09: Netflix lowers its subscriber estimates [8] and announces the DVD service will be renamed Qwikster [7].
- 2011-10: Netflix drops Qwikster but keeps the new prices [6].
- 2011-10: Netflix reports US subscribers fell from 24.6 million to 23.8 million in the quarter [9].
- 2013-02: All 13 episodes of House of Cards season one go live on the same day [11].
- 2013-04: Netflix adds 2 million US streaming members to reach 29.2 million, and shares jump 24% [12].

## Decision points
### D1 · 2000 · Sell to the giant, or fight alone?
- Situation: Netflix was a small DVD-by-mail company losing money, while Blockbuster earned about $800 million a year from late fees alone [1].
- Options a CEO realistically had (3, incl. what the company did): (a) offer to sell to Blockbuster and become its online arm; (b) keep fighting alone with outside investors; (c) shut down or shrink the business.
- What the company did: The founders offered to sell Netflix to Blockbuster for $50 million [1].
- What happened next: Blockbuster turned the offer down, and Netflix grew its flat-fee, no-late-fee mail service to 1 million subscribers within three years [1].

### D2 · 2007 · Start streaming while DVDs still work
- Situation: Netflix's DVD service was strong, with a catalog of over 70,000 DVD titles [3].
- Options a CEO realistically had (3, incl. what the company did): (a) wait until internet speeds improve; (b) launch streaming as a paid extra; (c) add streaming free inside the existing plans.
- What the company did: Netflix added instant streaming on PCs at no extra charge, with about 1,000 titles and viewing hours tied to the plan price [3].
- What happened next: By 2010 Netflix had 20 million subscribers, while Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy [1].

### D3 · 2010 · Push customers toward streaming with price
- Situation: A rival streaming service, Hulu Plus, had just cut its price to $7.99 [4].
- Options a CEO realistically had (3, incl. what the company did): (a) keep one bundled plan; (b) add a cheap streaming-only plan and raise DVD prices; (c) stop DVDs entirely.
- What the company did: Netflix launched a $7.99 streaming-only plan and raised the one-DVD unlimited plan from $8.99 to $9.99 [4].
- What happened next: Netflix ended 2010 with about 20 million subscribers and $160 million of net income [5].

### D4 · 2011 · Split the price, then split the company
- Situation: Netflix wanted to grow streaming faster, buy more streaming content and stop the DVD business slowing it down [8].
- Options a CEO realistically had (3, incl. what the company did): (a) keep the bundle and raise prices slowly; (b) split DVD and streaming into separate plans only; (c) split the plans and also spin DVDs into a separate brand.
- What the company did: Netflix made each service $7.99, so members who wanted both paid about $16 instead of $10 [6][8]. Two months later, the CEO apologized for poor communication and said DVDs would move to a new brand called Qwikster [7].
- What happened next: Reports said about 600,000 to 1 million members cancelled [6][7], and shares lost more than half their value since July [6].

### D5 · 2011 · Reverse Qwikster or push through?
- Situation: Customers and commentators mocked the Qwikster name, and the stock kept falling [6].
- Options a CEO realistically had (3, incl. what the company did): (a) push ahead with two websites; (b) drop Qwikster but keep the higher prices; (c) drop Qwikster and roll back prices.
- What the company did: Netflix dropped Qwikster within weeks and kept one website and one account, but kept the price rise [6].
- What happened next: US subscribers fell by about 800,000 in the quarter, and the company said the price rise mattered more than the branding [9].

### D6 · 2011 · Pay for its own shows
- Situation: Netflix depended on content licensed from studios and TV networks [9].
- Options a CEO realistically had (3, incl. what the company did): (a) keep licensing other people's shows; (b) order a pilot first, like TV networks; (c) commit big to a full series with no pilot.
- What the company did: Netflix outbid HBO and AMC for House of Cards and ordered two seasons, 26 episodes, without a pilot [10].
- What happened next: Netflix released the whole first season on one day in February 2013 [11], then added 2 million US members in the next quarter [12].

## What really happened
Blockbuster refused to buy Netflix for $50 million in 2000, and ten years later Blockbuster was bankrupt while Netflix had 20 million subscribers [1]. Netflix bet early on streaming by giving it away free inside DVD plans in 2007 [3]. In 2011 it moved too fast, raising the price for DVD plus streaming from about $10 to $16 and announcing Qwikster [6][7]. Netflix lost subscribers and most of its stock value in a few months [5][9]. It kept the new prices, spent more on content, and its own show House of Cards helped it grow again in 2013 [9][12].
