# Harshad Mehta: research brief
Hot Seat id: harshad-mehta · Years covered: 1980–1993 (epilogue to 2003) · Researched: 2026-10-29

## Sources
1. [Securities Scam: Genesis, Mechanics and Impact (Samir K. Barua and Jayanth R. Varma, IIM Ahmedabad working paper, September 1992)](https://www.iima.ac.in/sites/default/files/rnpfiles/1992-09-01jrvarma.pdf) (record: [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/iim/iimawp/wp01131.html)): the main source for how the money moved. Over Rs 3,500 crore moved from banks to brokers, April 1991 to May 1992. Government bond market Rs 3,000–4,000 crore a day, about a dozen RBI-approved brokers, money at 18–20%; stock market Rs 50–200 crore a day, about 500 BSE brokers, badla money at 35–40%. Ready forward (RF) explained: a secured loan for about 15 days, like a pawnbroker lending against jewellery. Bank receipt (BR) explained: an IOU for securities. IBA rules on BRs ("none … seriously adhered to"). Three steps that turned a bank-to-bank loan into an unsecured loan to a broker. The Janakiraman Committee put the banks' total exposure at Rs 3,542 crore, Rs 1,439 crore of it Harshad Mehta's (unsecured loans). Bank of Karad and Metropolitan Co-operative Bank fake BRs linked to the Narottam and Hiten Dalal group, not Mehta. Sensex about 1,000 (Feb 1991), below 2,000 until Feb 1992, peak about 4,500 (March 1992), fell to about 2,500, about 3,000 by Aug 1992. How it came out: RBI asked SBI for its holdings as on 31 March 1992; a Rs 574 crore gap; Rs 649 crore due from Mehta; he paid Rs 622 crore between 13 and 24 April 1992 by borrowing from NHB the same way, which left NHB with over Rs 1,200 crore at risk. Special Courts ordinance attached accused people's property and voided their deals of the past year ("tainted shares"). RF ban.

## Decision points
Background for the player (not a decision): a young man whose father ran a small textile business [17], an insurance clerk, then a jobber, who wins a seat on the Bombay Stock Exchange and opens his own firm, Grow More [9][17]. This works as the intro scene.

Two terms, explained simply (from [1]):
- **Ready forward (RF):** a short loan between two banks, usually about 15 days. One bank "sells" government bonds to another and buys them back later at a slightly higher price. The extra price is the interest. Like a pawnbroker lending against jewellery: the loan is safe because the lender holds the bonds.
- **Bank receipt (BR):** often the bonds never moved. The selling bank just gave a BR, a note saying "we have your bonds and will hand them over". An IOU. If no bonds stood behind it, the lender had no safety at all.

### D1 · 1990–91 · Where does the money come from?
- Situation: The stock market is rising and brokers need money to buy. Stock market money (badla) costs 35–40% a year; banks lend to each other at 18–20% [1]. Only about a dozen brokers work in the bank bond market, and you work in both markets [1]. Banks are under pressure to show profits [1].
- Options: (a) only trade with your own and your clients' money, and pay badla rates; grow slowly; (b) keep broking bank deals the proper way: match two banks, let them swap bonds and cheques directly, and take only your fee [1]; (c) ask banks to route their deal cheques through your firm, keep the money in your own account for a few days or weeks, and use it to buy shares.
- What he did: (c). Cheques drawn for one bank were credited to his account [1]; for NHB, SBI cheques of Rs 707.76 crore and Grindlays cheques of Rs 506.54 crore went into deals where no securities or BRs came back [6]; Maruti Udyog's cash reached his Grindlays account in early 1991 [4].
- What happened next: The Janakiraman Committee later traced Rs 1,439 crore of unsecured bank money to him [1]. A court later convicted him in the Maruti part (see D5) [4].
- Note: The fake Bank of Karad and Metropolitan Co-operative Bank BRs were mainly used by another broker group, not by Mehta [1]. Don't put them in his story.

### D2 · 1991–92 · Ride the ACC rally?
- Situation: The Sensex rose from about 1,000 (Feb 1991) to about 4,500 (March 1992) [1]. Mehta bought heavily and argued that old companies like ACC were worth what it would cost to build them again (his "replacement cost" idea) [17]. ACC shares went to around Rs 9,000–10,000 [12][17]. Newspapers called him the "Big Bull" [9][12].
- Options: (a) sell part of your shares, pay back the banks, and lock in the gains; (b) spread your money across many shares and slow down; (c) keep buying ACC and other favourites with bank money and keep telling the market why prices should go higher.
- What he did: (c). He kept buying with bank money; well over Rs 1,000 crore was in the market [9][1].
- What happened next: His bank money now had to be returned on short deadlines while his wealth sat in shares [1]. He paid Rs 28 crore advance tax for 1991–92, which drew the tax office's attention [9].

### D3 · April 1992 · SBI wants its money now
- Situation: RBI asked SBI to list its bonds as on 31 March 1992. SBI found a Rs 574 crore gap, and then that Rs 649 crore was due from Mehta. SBI called him in and asked for the bonds or the money [1].
- Options: (a) sell shares, even at a loss, to repay SBI, and tell the bank the truth; (b) say you cannot pay and ask for time; (c) borrow from another bank the same way, through a deal with no real security, and use that to pay SBI.
- What he did: (c). He paid SBI Rs 622 crore between 13 and 24 April 1992, by "borrowing" from NHB the same way he had borrowed before [1].
- What happened next: NHB was left with over Rs 1,200 crore at risk [1]. On 23 April 1992 Sucheta Dalal's Times of India column reported it [12]. Within about a month, investigators found a diversion of over Rs 3,500 crore [1]. Shares fell over 40% in under two months [10]; the Sensex hit about 2,500 [1][9].

### D4 · June–Nov 1992 · The investigation closes in
- Situation: A Special Court ordinance (6 June 1992) lets a Custodian attach the property of notified people and covers all deals since 1 April 1991 [14]. Shares that passed through accused brokers became "tainted", which made the crash worse [1]. NHB went to the CBI in July 1992 [6]; Parliament set up a JPC in August 1992 [23].
- Options: (a) admit what happened, hand over records, and help the Custodian sell shares to repay banks; (b) say you can repay everything if you are allowed to trade, and fight each case; (c) hide assets and stay quiet.
- What he did: (b). He claimed he could clear all his dues if allowed to [1], and fought the cases [10][9].
- What happened next: He spent 100 days in jail before bail [7]. He faced 72 criminal offences and over 600 civil suits [10]. SEBI later banned him for life from the market [17].

### D5 · June 1993 · Accuse the Prime Minister?
- Situation: He is on bail with many cases ahead [7]. A parliamentary committee is questioning him [7].
- Options: (a) keep quiet and fight in court; (b) go public and say you paid the Prime Minister Rs 1 crore; (c) give what you claim to the JPC in private and let it judge.
- What he did: (b). On 16 June 1993 he made the charge, and on 28 June he held a press conference with a video of how cash would fit in a suitcase [7].
- What happened next: Rao said the affidavit was false; security logbooks said he was not at home [7]. A preliminary inquiry found no prima facie evidence and no case was registered [8]. Treat this as an allegation only. Nothing in our sources shows it helped his cases.

(Optional D6, epilogue rather than a decision: 1999 conviction, appeal, death in 2001, conviction confirmed 2003 [4][5].)

### Taster recommendation
Use **D1 (where the money comes from)** and **D2 (ride the ACC rally)** as the 1-minute taster. They are the heart of the story: the honest slow route against the shortcut, then the high of the bull run. Both are well sourced [1][9][12][17]. Neither needs court cases, the PM claim or living family members. A 1-minute ending can jump straight to April 1992: the SBI gap, the exposé and the crash [1][12].

## What really happened
The money came out of the banks, not the market. Per the Janakiraman Committee, banks had Rs 3,542 crore at risk; Rs 1,439 crore of it was traced to Mehta [1]. The JPC put the scam at about Rs 4,400 crore [11]; other press figures are about Rs 3,500–4,000 crore [10][12]. When it broke in April 1992, the Sensex fell from about 4,500 to about 2,500 [1], losing over Rs 1 lakh crore of market value [9]. He faced 72 criminal offences and over 600 civil suits [10]; 27 cases were still pending when he died [9]. He was convicted in the Maruti Udyog case (Rs 38.97 crore): five years' rigorous imprisonment from the Special Court in 1999 [4][5]. He died in custody in Thane on 31 December 2001, aged 47 [4][17]. In 2003 the Supreme Court confirmed that conviction, 2–1 [4]. His brother and a cousin were convicted in the NHB case in 2016 [6]. The Custodian has since paid over Rs 4,000 crore from Mehta Group assets to the tax office and banks [10].

What changed: RBI banned banks' ready forward deals in June 1992 and later allowed them back under tight rules [2][3]; the Janakiraman Committee asked for tight BR rules and broker registration [3]; SEBI (given legal powers in 1992) added broker rules [13]; NSE started screen trading in 1994, BSE in 1995, and paperless shares followed in 1996 [13][19][20].

## Risks
- **Living people.** Sucheta Dalal and Debashis Basu (journalists); Ashwin, Sudhir, Jyoti and Deepak Mehta (family); C. Rangarajan; the MP's son named in [7] (do not name him). Use roles, not names, in the story, and nothing about anyone's private life.
- **Pending matters.** Family tax appeals and Custodian disputes were still running in 2019–2022 [15][16]; the 2011 payouts were interim and could be reversed [10][11]; the 2016 NHB convictions could be appealed to the Supreme Court [6]. We could not confirm the status today. Do not say anything about the family's money or guilt.
- **Legal claims: alleged vs. found.**
  - Diverting bank money (NHB, SBI): alleged by CBI (complaint 10 July 1992) [6]. No verdict for Mehta: the case ended (abated) in April 2002 because he died [6]. Safe: "Investigators said…", "the Janakiraman Committee traced Rs 1,439 crore of unsecured bank money to him" [1].
  - Maruti Udyog funds (Rs 38.97 crore): found. Special Court, Mumbai, 27/28 Sept 1999, convicted under Section 403 IPC (dishonest misappropriation) and conspiracy; Supreme Court, 14 Jan 2003, confirmed by 2–1 [4][5]. Safe: "A court convicted him for misusing Maruti Udyog's money; the Supreme Court upheld it."
  - Share theft (2.8 million shares, Nov 1992 arrest): only in Wikipedia [18]; no outcome found. Leave out.
  - Rs 1 crore to the PM (1993): Mehta's allegation only. Rao denied it [7]; a preliminary inquiry found no prima facie evidence; no case registered [8]. Safe: "He claimed he had paid the Prime Minister. An inquiry found no evidence." Never show a payment as fact, never show the PM, never show the suitcase as real.
  - "Fake BRs" from Bank of Karad and Metropolitan Co-op: tied to another group, not Mehta [1]. Many blogs get this wrong. Don't use it for him.
  - His death: some say he was murdered; this is a rumour [17]. Say only "died in custody after chest pain / heart trouble" or simply "died in 2001".
- **Myth to avoid.** "The scam created SEBI's powers." The SEBI Act came in early 1992, before the scam broke [13]. Say "SEBI got stronger rules" instead.
- **Numbers vary.** Scam size: Rs 3,542 crore (Janakiraman, banks' exposure [1]), Rs 3,500 crore [1][10], Rs 4,000 crore [12], Rs 4,400 crore (JPC [11]). Use "over Rs 3,500 crore" and name the source. Mehta's share: Rs 1,439 crore (Janakiraman [1]); the breakdown table in [1] adds to Rs 1,567 crore. Number of cases: 72 criminal / 600 civil [10] vs 27 pending [9] vs "27 charges, 4 convictions" [17].
- **Thin sources.** ACC price: only Wikipedia (Rs 200 to nearly Rs 9,000) [17] and one press piece (bid up to Rs 10,000) [12]. Say "ACC shares went from a few hundred rupees to thousands" or "around Rs 9,000". Early career (card auction, jobber, Grow More) is only in Wikipedia [17].
- **Tone.** The story must not teach fraud as a how-to. Show what it cost: banks, savers, jail.
