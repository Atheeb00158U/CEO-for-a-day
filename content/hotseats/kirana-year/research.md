# A kirana's year: design brief
Hot Seat id: kirana-year · Years covered: one made-up year, April to March · Researched: 2026-10-30
Complexity 1 · Indian: yes · small · Gate for Module 1.3 · Onboarding taster (d1 and d2)

> **Made up.** This is not a real company. Asha, her shop and her town are invented: a composite built from
> what many real kiranas face. It is the only Hot Seat allowed to break the real-companies rule (approved by
> Atheeb). The app must say so in plain words, every time: on the map card, on the first screen, and on the
> verdict sheet. Suggested line: **"Made-up story, based on many real kiranas."** It must never show the
> "Real history" or "Our estimate" tags, and never a "What the company really did" panel (see *Risks*).

## Realism facts the story leans on
- A kirana earns a little on each packet and more on loose goods it weighs and packs itself [1]. Story: a ₹10 biscuit pack earns ₹1; a ₹10 bag of loose namkeen earns ₹3. (See open question 1 about the lesson's ₹4.)
- After rent, power, wastage and unpaid udhaar, the owner keeps only a small slice of sales [1].
- Credit (udhaar) is part of what a kirana *is* [2]. It is many small amounts from the same families, paid back around month-end or salary week, and the late payers creep up [3].
- Customers still like their kirana and plan to keep shopping there, but they want more variety [2].
- Paying by QR code at the counter is normal now [6].
- The rain's arrival moves by a week or more from year to year [7]; rainwear sells only when rain actually falls [8].
- Heavy rain keeps shoppers home and shortens shop hours [9]. *Applied to a grocer, this is our estimate: a grocer sells needs, not wants, so the drop is smaller than for a cloth shop.*
- The big festive rush runs from Navratri to Diwali and on to Chhath [10]; a kirana must buy extra stock before it.
- 10-minute delivery apps are reaching tier-2 towns [13], with many more items than a kirana [13]; some buyers move part of their shopping [12]. How badly kiranas are hurt is disputed [11].
- GST: a shop selling only goods, with turnover up to ₹40 lakh, need not register [5]. If it registers, the composition scheme (1% of turnover, up to ₹1.5 crore) keeps the paperwork light [4]. **The story keeps Asha's shop under ₹40 lakh a year and has no GST decision** (GST is not a Unit 1 lesson). One line in the intro can say "too small for GST forms" only if open question 3 clears.

## The person, the goal and the clock
- **Who:** You are **Asha Kulkarni, 27**. Your father, Baba, ran *Kulkarni General Stores* for 30 years. His knees are bad, so this April he hands you the keys. (All names made up.)
- **The shop:** one room, about 1,000 items on the shelves [2], a QR code at the counter [6], a fat udhaar notebook. Sales about ₹3 lakh a month; most of it goes back to the wholesaler.
- **The town:** a busy lane in **Kesarganj**, a made-up mid-sized town in Madhya Pradesh. (Not in a state excluded in [5]. Monsoon usually arrives here in mid to late June: see open question 5.)
- **The goal:** by 31 March, every wholesaler bill paid, the ₹60,000 rainy-day fund still in the box, and the regulars still coming.
- **The clock:** one year, April to March. Baba says: "One year. If the shop can't pay its way, we rent it out to the phone shop next door."
- **Meters:** *Profit* → **"Cash box"** (money in hand today). *Future* → **"Regulars"** (how loyal and steady your customers are). Endings route by Regulars.
- **Board trust (3 hearts):** shown as **"Baba's trust"** if the label can change (open question 8). Losing all three means Baba takes back the keys.

Opening scenes (two at most):
- `intro` (April): *"April, Kesarganj. Your father's kirana is yours now. Baba gives you the keys, the udhaar notebook and ₹60,000 saved for bad months. 'One year,' he says. 'Pay every bill. Keep the regulars. Or we rent the shop out.'"*
- (Optional second scene) the first-day numbers: *"You sell about ₹10,000 a day. But most of it goes back to the wholesaler. What is left for you is small."* This line sets up lesson 1.1.1.

### d1 · April · The front shelf (onboarding call 1; very gentle)
- **Situation:** The shelf by the counter is the best spot: every customer sees it. A biscuit salesman offers a free rack if you fill it with his ₹10 packs; they sell fast, but each earns you ₹1. A ₹10 bag of namkeen you weigh and pack yourself earns ₹3, but sells slower [1]. The setup gives the player both numbers, so only 1.1.1 is needed.
- **Question:** "What goes on the front shelf?"
- **Advisor (Baba):** "Fast sellers keep the counter busy. But a busy counter is not the same as money in the box."
- **Options:**
  1. **Cheapest prices up front** — "Put the cheapest deals in front to pull in crowds, even if you earn almost nothing on them." → *costly*. More people, more bills, almost no profit; you work harder for the same money. *Why:* sales are not profit (1.1.1). *Better:* put the items that earn the most where people look first.
  2. **Your own namkeen and dry fruit up front** — "Put your own packed namkeen in front. It earns ₹3 a bag, but it sells slower." → **great**. Sales barely move, but the cash box fills faster; regulars like the fresh namkeen. *Why:* know your margin on every item (1.1.2).
  3. **The salesman's biscuit rack** — "Take the free rack of fast biscuits. They fly off the shelf, but earn ₹1 a pack." → *risky*. The counter is busy and sales jump, but you keep little more than before. *Why:* fast sellers bring people in; they don't pay the bills. *Better:* keep fast sellers nearby and put high-margin items in front.
- **Flags:** `shelf_margin` / `shelf_fast` / `shelf_cheap` (used in the d2 setup: how much cash you have).

### d2 · May · "Write it in the book" (onboarding call 2; gentle)
- **Situation:** Udhaar is part of every kirana [2]. Baba's notebook is full: small amounts from the same families, paid back "at month-end" [3]. Now ₹40,000 is owed to you, and the wholesaler wants his money within two weeks. Three regulars ask for more.
- **Question:** "What do you do about udhaar?"
- **Advisor (Baba):** "These families have shopped here for years. But the wholesaler does not take promises."
- **Options:**
  1. **Yes to every regular** — "Keep giving udhaar to every regular, like Baba did. They are loyal, and they always pay in the end." → *costly*. Udhaar climbs to ₹60,000; you pay the wholesaler late and he cuts your credit. *Why:* udhaar is a loan you give, and loans need limits (1.2.2). *Better:* a limit per family and a fixed day to pay.
  2. **Stop all udhaar** — "Stop udhaar for everyone. Cash or QR only, even if some regulars walk to the next shop." → *risky*. Cash comes in, but two good families move to the shop down the lane. *Why:* credit is one reason people choose a kirana [2]; cutting it off loses good payers too. *Better:* keep credit for good payers, with limits.
  3. **A limit and a day** — "Give each family a limit and one day a month to pay. No new udhaar until the old is cleared." → **great**. A few people grumble; most pay on the first Sunday. You pay the wholesaler on time. *Why:* treat udhaar like a loan: limit, due date, a written record (1.2.2).
- **Flags:** `udhaar_open` / `udhaar_stop` / `udhaar_limit`.
- **Onboarding end:** after d2's verdict, onboarding stops. A one-line cliffhanger works well here: *"June comes. Your umbrellas are stacked to the roof. The sky stays blue."*

### Bridge scene · June
*"Every year Baba bought umbrellas and raincoats in May, before the rain. You did the same. June ends, and the sky stays dry. The rain is late this year: three weeks late."* [7] (Path variant: if `udhaar_open`, add *"and the cash box is thin."*)

### d3 · July · The late rain
- **Situation:** ₹25,000 of umbrellas and raincoats sit unsold. Baba's notes show that in most years they sold out by mid-July. This year the rain is late [7][8]. Baba is upset: "Never again."
- **Question:** "What do you do with the rain stock?"
- **Advisor (Baba):** "I bought them the same way for twenty years. This is the first time it went this wrong."
- **Options:**
  1. **Keep the method** — "Keep the stock and your method. Most years it worked; this was bad luck. Next year, buy in two smaller lots." → **great**. In late July the rain comes hard; umbrellas sell out in ten days [8]. *Why:* judge the thinking, not the luck (1.3.1).
  2. **Sell it off and stop** — "Sell the rain stock at cost today and never stock it again. It was a bad bet." → *costly*. You lose the margin; the rain comes a week later and your regulars buy umbrellas elsewhere. *Why:* one bad outcome doesn't make a bad decision; and money already spent is gone, so decide on what comes next (1.3.4). *Better:* check whether the method was sound before you drop it.
  3. **Return it, buy late** — "Send the stock back at a small loss. From now on, buy only after the rain starts." → *risky*. Safer cash today, but next year you'll be buying when every shop wants the same stock. *Why:* waiting for certainty costs you the best days. *Better:* keep the method; just split the order.
- **Flags:** `rain_keep` / `rain_sold` / `rain_return`.

### d4 · August · The wedding and the slow months
- **Situation:** Heavy rain keeps people home; shops close early [9]; your walk-ins are down (our estimate: less than a cloth shop, since people still need food). Diwali stock must be bought in September [10]. Then your cousin's wedding is fixed for November, and the family asks for ₹50,000 "from the shop money". The rainy-day fund is ₹60,000: about three slow months. (Path variants: if `udhaar_open`, the fund is already down to ₹40,000; if `rain_sold`, the fund is short by the lost margin.)
- **Question:** "Where does the wedding money come from?"
- **Advisor (Baba):** "Family is family. But the shop is what feeds the family."
- **Options:**
  1. **From the cash box** — "Give the ₹50,000 from the shop's fund. Family first. Diwali sales will refill it." → *costly*. In September you can't pay for full Diwali stock; the shelves look thin in the best weeks of the year. *Why:* keep shop money and home money apart (1.2.4). *Better:* pay family costs from your own wage and home savings.
  2. **From the fund, then borrow** — "Give it from the fund, then take a short loan to buy Diwali stock. The rush will pay it back." → *risky*. Diwali sells well, but the interest eats a big part of the extra profit, and the fund is gone for the slow months. *Why:* a buffer is for the shop's bad months (1.2.5). *Better:* keep the fund whole and find the wedding money at home.
  3. **Keep shop money apart** — "Say no to the shop fund. Pay yourself a fixed wage, and give what you can from that and home savings." → **great**. The family is upset for a week. In September you buy full Diwali stock, and the shop has its best festival in years [10]. *Why:* shop money and home money are separate (1.2.4); the buffer carries you through slow months (1.2.5).
- **Flags:** `fund_spent` / `fund_loan` / `fund_kept`.

### Bridge scene · Diwali to December
Variants by `fund_*`: full shelves and a record Diwali; or a good Diwali with a loan to pay; or thin shelves while the shop across the road is packed. Then: *"In December, a new board goes up two streets away: a 10-minute delivery store."* [13]

### d5 · January · The 10-minute app
- **Situation:** A quick-delivery store opens nearby; it has thousands more items than you [13] and free delivery for now [13]. Some families now order part of their shopping on the app [12]. Your Sunday sales are down. (Path variants: if `udhaar_stop`, two regulars you lost in May are already on the app; if `udhaar_limit`, your credit customers still come, because the app gives no credit [2].)
- **Question:** "How do you answer the app?"
- **Advisor (Baba):** "Big shops came before, and we are still here. But I never saw anything this fast."
- **Options:**
  1. **Wait it out** — "Do nothing new. Your regulars know you, and the app's free delivery can't last." → *costly*. The app keeps its offers longer than you hoped; slowly, young families stop coming. *Why:* hoping is not a plan; you didn't test anything (1.3.2). *Better:* try a small answer first.
  2. **Test one lane** — "Try free delivery on WhatsApp in your own lane for one month. Count the orders, then decide." → **great**. Small cost: your nephew on a cycle after school. Forty orders in the first month; you add two more lanes. *Why:* test small before you bet big (1.3.2).
  3. **Go all in** — "Go all in: a scooter, a delivery boy and leaflets for the whole town, at app prices." → *risky*. Orders come, but at app prices you lose money on most of them; the scooter loan bites. *Why:* betting big before you know what works; every rupee there was not spent elsewhere (1.3.5). *Better:* test small, then grow what works.
- **Flags:** `app_wait` / `app_test` / `app_allin`.

## Endings (31 March; route by Regulars)
- **Legend** (Regulars ≥ 70): every bill paid, the fund bigger than in April, home delivery in three lanes. Baba hangs your name on the board above the shop.
- **Survivor** (≥ 40): the shop is still open and the bills are paid, but the fund is thin and the app has taken some families. Baba says: "One more year."
- **Collapse** (< 40): the wholesaler wants cash up front, the fund is gone, and half the shelves are empty. The family rents half the shop to the phone shop next door.
- **Fired** (all hearts lost): Baba takes the keys back before March. "Not this year, beta."
- `realEnding`: none (see Risks). Variants for the main paths: "you built delivery", "you kept the fund", "you kept udhaar open".

## Risks
- **The app assumes a real story.** The schema needs `realHistory` and `realEnding`; the player shows "Real history" / "Our estimate" tags and "What the company really did"; `hotseat:check` asks for a stamped `factcheck.md`. With no `real` option anywhere, the check treats every call as off the real path, so every screen would carry "Our estimate". This needs a small `madeUp: true` flag that hides those tags and panels, shows the made-up label, and swaps the fact-check for a realism check of this file. That is engineering work and a rule change in `AUTHORING.md`; Atheeb should approve it.
- **The onboarding trial is still Kingfisher.** `TRIAL_ID` in `src/content/catalog.ts` is `kingfisher-airlines`. Making this the taster means changing it, and checking that the trial stops cleanly after d2.
- **Lessons give away the answers.** CURRICULUM's 1.3.1 example is "Raju stocked umbrellas and the rain came late", which is d3 almost word for word; 1.2.2's example (limit + collection day) is d2's great option. The course says lessons must never give away a gate. Change those examples, or change d2/d3.
- **Weak sources for the money numbers.** The margin bands [1] come from a vendor page with no data behind them; the udhaar pattern [3] is vendor marketing. Good enough for a made-up shop, not for any "fact" line.
- **Quick-commerce harm is disputed** [11]. The story shows a threat to *one* shop, never a claim that apps are killing kiranas.
- **Rain and footfall:** our sources on rain cutting sales are about cloth and bangle markets [9], not grocers. The text keeps the drop mild.
- **Tone:** d4 must not read as "family is bad". The great option still gives money, from the right pocket.
- **Copy passes, path reads and a fresh-context review** still apply, even with no fact-check.
