## Decision points
### D1 · 2000 · Buy the little mail-order startup?
- Situation: Netflix rented DVDs by post, was losing money, and asked Blockbuster for $50 million to buy it [1].
- Options a CEO realistically had (3, incl. what the company did): (a) buy Netflix for $50M; (b) partner with Netflix or build an online rental arm yourself; (c) say no and focus on stores.
- What the company did: Said no; the CEO saw Netflix as a niche business, according to a Netflix founder's memoir [1].
- What happened next: Blockbuster did not launch its own US online rental service until August 2004 [2].

### D2 · 2004 · Launch online rental to fight Netflix
- Situation: Same-store sales had fallen 5.1% in the first half of 2004, and online rentals were a growing market [4].
- Options: (a) launch online rental priced below Netflix; (b) buy an online player; (c) stay a store-only business.
- What the company did: Launched Blockbuster Online in August 2004 at $19.99 a month, about $2 below Netflix, with no late fees [4].
- What happened next: It reached 1 million subscribers in under a year [8], but analysts estimated online would add $170 million to 2005 spending [5].

### D3 · 2004 · Kill late fees
- Situation: Late fees ("extended viewing fees") were about 13% of rental revenue in Q3 2004 [8], and were expected to bring $250–300 million in operating income in 2005 [2].
- Options: (a) keep late fees; (b) drop them everywhere; (c) test in a few cities first.
- What the company did: Dropped late fees in US stores from January 1, 2005, with a one-week grace period after which the customer bought the item [3].
- What happened next: In Q1 2005, late-fee revenue fell $128.5 million and the company lost $57.5 million [6].

### D4 · 2005 · Fight the activist investor
- Situation: An activist investor held about 9.7% of Class A shares and wanted lower spending and more cash back to shareholders, attacking the late-fee and online spending [5].
- Options: (a) fight the activist's board slate; (b) give him board seats and cut spending; (c) sell or merge the company.
- What the company did: Fought the proxy contest; the CEO said he would leave if he lost his board seat [5].
- What happened next: The activist's slate won; the CEO stayed as CEO and the board said it would put him back as chairman [7].

### D5 · 2006 · Total Access: use the stores as a weapon
- Situation: Netflix had about 3 million online customers against Blockbuster's expected 1 million in mid-2005 [5].
- Options: (a) keep online and stores separate; (b) link them so stores add value to online members; (c) cut online spending to save cash.
- What the company did: Launched Total Access on November 1, 2006, letting members swap mailed discs for free in-store rentals, from $5.99 a month [9].
- What happened next: Subscribers grew strongly, but launch costs were higher than expected [10]; free exchanges cost $29 million in Q3 2007 alone [11].

### D6 · 2007–2008 · New CEO: save cash or double down on digital?
- Situation: The old CEO left after a pay fight with the board [10], and Blockbuster had lost over $4 billion from 2002 to 2005 [11].
- Options: (a) keep investing in Total Access; (b) cut online costs and reinvent the store; (c) buy a bigger retail partner.
- What the company did: The new CEO charged $7 extra a month for in-store exchanges, tested new store formats and electronics [11], closed the Movielink purchase [12], and bid for Circuit City before withdrawing [13].
- What happened next: Blockbuster lost 500,000 online subscribers in summer 2007 and its shares fell 17% after the November 2007 investor meeting [11].

## What really happened
By late 2009 Blockbuster's share price was below $1, and in July 2010 the NYSE suspended trading [14]. On September 23, 2010, Blockbuster filed for Chapter 11 with a plan to cut debt from nearly $1 billion to about $100 million, while its 3,000 US stores stayed open [15]. Reports named Netflix and Redbox as the rivals that had overtaken it [16]. In April 2011, DISH Network won the bankruptcy auction for Blockbuster's assets with a bid worth about $320 million, gaining more than 1,700 stores [17].

## Risks
- Living people central to the story: the former CEO, the activist investor, the later CEO and both Netflix founders are alive; describe them by role and avoid blame language.
- The 2000 meeting is told mainly from the Netflix side (a founder's memoir and tweets) [1]; do not present the "laughed" anecdote or the CEO's thinking as fact.
- The Blockbuster name and logo belong to DISH (now part of EchoStar) after 2011 [17]; avoid using the logo or branded ads. Current litigation involving the brand owner was not checked.
- Movielink price conflicts: $6.6 million in the SEC filing [12] vs $7.7 million in press [11]; use the SEC figure.
- No-late-fees ads led to a 2005 settlement with state attorneys general (seen in search results only, not opened, so not cited).
- Total Access subscriber totals are thin; no official peak count was confirmed.
