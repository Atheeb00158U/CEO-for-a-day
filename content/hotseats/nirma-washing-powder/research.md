## Timeline
- About 1966: Karsanbhai Patel, from a farming family in north Gujarat, finishes a BSc in chemistry; he works as a lab technician, first at a cotton mill and then in the Gujarat government's Geology and Mining Department [2].
- Before 1969: Detergent powder is a premium product; most homes wash with laundry soap bars [4][6][11]. Hindustan Lever's Surf leads the powder market [3][5].
- 1969: Patel starts making a phosphate-free yellow detergent powder at home, packing it in a 10x10 ft room [1][2][6].
- 1969: He prices it at ₹3.50 a kg; Surf costs about ₹13 a kg [1][3][6][8]. (Some sources say ₹3 [5][7].)
- 1969 onwards: He sells door to door by bicycle after office hours, about 15–20 packets a day on a 15 km ride, with a money-back promise [1][2][5][7].
- About 1972: He leaves his government job to run the business full time [2][7]. Later (date unknown) he sets up a small workshop in an Ahmedabad suburb [2].
- 1970s: Nirma sells through one line of distributors who supply shops directly, and goes to smaller towns rather than the rich areas where Surf is strong [4][7]. Costs are kept very low: local raw materials bought for cash at a discount, plain plastic pouches, its own printing press [3][4][6].
- 1975: The "Washing powder Nirma" jingle first plays on radio [6]. HBS dates the hit to Surf from "after 1975" [11].
- Date unclear (1970s or early 1980s): Shops want credit; payments come late and dues pile up [3][5][7]. When cash from shops suddenly drops, Patel pulls back over 90% of his stock from the market while the ads keep running; after about a month shops agree to pay cash upfront, and supply resumes [3][5][10].
- 1982: Nirma's first TV ad [6].
- 1985: Nirma passes Surf as India's best-selling detergent [4][6][7].
- 1985–86: Hindustan Lever runs Project STING ("Strategy to Inhibit Nirma's Growth") [12]; see also [4][6][7].
- 1988: Nirma holds about 60% of the detergent market, selling over 1.72 lakh tonnes [3][4][6][7]. Hindustan Lever launches Wheel as its low-price answer [4][7][14] (date disputed; see Risks).
- 1994: Nirma Ltd goes public [8][9].
- Mid-to-late 1990s: Nirma builds its own raw material plants: a linear alkyl benzene (LAB) plant near Vadodara and a soda ash plant near Bhavnagar; it expects detergent costs to fall a further 25–30% [8]. The founder says this was planned from the start [9].

## Decision points

### D1 · 1969 · The price
- Situation: You make a washing powder at home. Surf, the big brand, costs about ₹13 a kg [1][6]. Most families can't pay that and wash with soap bars [4][6][11].
- Options: (a) price close to Surf, so buyers trust it as a "quality" powder; (b) price a little under Surf and win some of Surf's buyers; (c) price at about a quarter of Surf, near what families pay for soap, and sell a lot.
- What he did: (c). ₹3.50 a kg [1][3][6][8].
- What happened next: It sold well in Ahmedabad [5]; HBS says the low price opened a new market of soap-bar users and "decimated" Surf after 1975 [11]; Nirma passed Surf in 1985 [6].
- Tests: **1.1.5** (thin margin works only with high sales and low costs) and **1.1.1** (sales are not profit: a cheap price only works if costs are cheaper still [4]). Supported.
- Note: no source gives Nirma's cost or margin per kg in 1969. The story can say the margin was thin; it cannot give a number.

### D2 · 1969–72 · Where to make it
- Situation: Demand is small and you still have a government job [2].
- Options: (a) keep making it at home in a small room and sell after office; (b) quit and rent a small factory now, so you can make more; (c) pay a local maker to produce it for you under your name.
- What he did: (a). He made and packed it at home [1][2][6], kept his job for about three years [2][7], and only later set up a small workshop in an Ahmedabad suburb [2].
- What happened next: He went full time around 1972 [2][7]; the business grew into distributors and shops [4].
- Tests: **1.1.4** (break-even: low rent means few sales are needed to cover costs) and **1.1.3** (fixed vs variable costs). Supported in spirit: the facts (home room, kept the job) are sourced, but no source gives costs or a break-even number. The lesson is drawn from the facts, not stated by any source.
- Option (c) is invented for balance; nothing says he considered it.

### D3 · 1970s–early 1980s · Shops want credit
- Situation: Shops now stock Nirma, but they want credit and pay late, even after the stock sells [3][5][7]. Cash coming in drops suddenly [10]. You need cash to buy raw materials.
- Options: (a) keep giving credit, since shops are where the buyers are; (b) take back most of your stock, keep the ads running, and sell again only for cash; (c) borrow from a bank to cover the gap and wait for shops to pay.
- What he did: (b). He pulled back over 90% of his stock while the ads kept running [10]; buyers asked for Nirma and found none for about a month [5]; shops then paid cash upfront [3].
- What happened next: Supply resumed and Nirma went on to pass Surf by 1985 [5][6].
- Tests: **1.2.2** (udhaar is a loan you give) and **1.2.1** (profit on paper, empty cash box). Supported.
- Conflict to resolve before drafting: [5] says Patel avoided credit from the start; [7] says he gave credit, lost money and took packets back; [3] and [10] describe dues and the stock pull-back. Safest real-path wording: "shops owed you money and cash was short; you pulled the stock back and asked for cash". The curriculum's framing ("door to door for cash, or give stock to shops on credit") is only partly supported: door-to-door cash selling is sourced [1][2], but no source shows a moment where he chose between the two. Recommend the stock pull-back as the real decision.
- Date is unclear (radio ads began 1975, TV 1982 [6]). One unopened source (YourStory citing Business Standard) puts it in the early 1980s. Avoid a year; keep the scene "years later".

### D4 · Mid-1990s · Make your own raw materials? (optional or moved)
- Situation: Nirma leads detergents, but Hindustan Lever spends far more on ads [8] and the main inputs (LAB and soda ash) are bought from others [8][9].
- Options: (a) keep buying raw materials and spend on ads instead; (b) build your own LAB and soda ash plants, a big cost now for cheaper powder later; (c) cut the pack size and quality to protect the price.
- What it did: (b). A LAB plant near Vadodara and a soda ash plant near Bhavnagar (about ₹1,037 crore) [8]; costs expected to fall a further 25–30% [8]; the founder said it was planned from the start [9].
- What happened next: not fully sourced. Nirma is today one of India's largest soda ash makers (search snippet from its 2024–25 annual report, not opened).
- Tests: **1.1.3** (a big fixed cost to cut the variable cost of every kilo). Supported, **but outside the curriculum's 1969–mid-1980s window**. In the 1970s–80s, the sourced cost moves are smaller: cash discounts from local suppliers, plain pouches, own printing press [3][4]. Choose one: (i) keep D4 in the 1990s and stretch the Hot Seat's years, or (ii) set D4 in the 1970s as "cut costs: cheap plain pouches and cash-discount buying vs fancy boxes" using [3][4][6]. Option (ii) is thinner and undated.
- The curriculum note says "making its own inputs is ✓ in curriculum.md". That ✓ rests on [3] (The Strategy Story), which gives no date. The dated evidence [8] puts it in the 1990s.

### D5 · 1975–82 · Spend on ads? (optional)
- Situation: Nirma sells through distributors and shops but is still a small name against Surf.
- Options: (a) no ads; the price sells itself; (b) a jingle on radio, then TV, at much lower spend than the big brand; (c) match the big brand's ad spend.
- What it did: (b). Radio jingle 1975, first TV ad 1982, made by a Gujarat agency [4][6]; later spend was far below Lever's [8].
- What happened next: Nirma passed Surf in 1985 [6].
- Tests: none of the six assigned codes directly. Use only if the Hot Seat needs a fifth call; it also shows that cheap selling still needs demand (a bridge to D3).

### Rival's view (for a late scene, not a decision)
Hindustan Lever avoided cutting Surf's price [4], ran Project STING in 1985–86 [12], and launched the cheaper Wheel [4][14] after a long delay [11]. This is the `hul-wheel` Daily Call's story; mention it only as what happened next.

## What really happened
A government chemist made a cheap yellow washing powder at home in 1969 and priced it at about a quarter of Surf [1][6]. He sold it door to door after work, kept costs very low, and went full time around 1972 [2][3]. When shops paid late, he pulled his stock back and got them to pay cash [3][10]. Radio and TV ads made the jingle famous [6]. Nirma passed Surf in 1985 and had about 60% of the market by 1988 [6]. Hindustan Lever answered late with Wheel [4][11]. In the 1990s Nirma went public and built its own raw material plants [8][9].

Ending tier: **legend** (beat the market leader in about 16 years from a home room).
