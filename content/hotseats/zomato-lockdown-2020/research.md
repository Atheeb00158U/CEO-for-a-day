## Decision points
### D1 · late March 2020 · Orders crash overnight
- Situation: Lockdown from 25 March [6]; GMV down 80% in a week [9]; only about a fifth of restaurants open [5]; a lot of new money had just come in (Jan 2020) [2].
- Options: (a) wait it out and change nothing; the bank balance is healthy; (b) ask staff for voluntary pay cuts now, and help delivery partners; (c) cut a large share of jobs in the first week.
- What they did: (b). Voluntary pay cuts by hundreds of staff, a delivery partner fund, and food kits for daily wage workers [4][7].
- What happened next: Orders stayed far below normal through April and May [2][5]. Voluntary cuts did not reach the company's target [1].
- Tests: 7.2.5 (in a crisis, decide fast). Quiz only in the curriculum; this is a natural first beat.

### D2 · May 2020 · One deep cut, or small cuts month by month?
- Situation: Seven weeks of lockdown. The company expects a quarter to two-fifths of restaurants to close for good [1]. It says it has cash, but wants to save more in case it gets worse [1].
- Options: (a) one plan, all at once: cut about 13% of jobs, cut pay across the company, and shed offices; (b) trim a few roles each month and see how things go; (c) no job cuts; only deeper pay cuts for everyone.
- What they did: (a). One note on 15 May covered layoffs, pay cuts and office cuts together [1][2][3].
- What happened next: No second layoff round was found in our sources for 2020 (see Open questions). By July the business was at 60% of pre-COVID and burning under US$1 million a month [9][10].
- Tests: 7.2.2 (one deep cut is kinder than many small ones). **Supported**, with one caveat: we can say Zomato made one announcement; we cannot prove there was never a later cut.

### D3 · May 2020 · How you let people go
- Situation: About 13% of staff, more than 500 people [1][2]. Some work through agencies, with only 15 days' notice in their contracts [1].
- Options: (a) a short email and the legal minimum notice; (b) personal video calls from leaders, half pay for six months, health cover, help finding jobs, keep their laptops; (c) keep everyone on the books at full pay with no real work until business returns.
- What they did: (b). Video calls within 24 hours; others told within 6 hours their jobs were safe; 50% salary for 6 months or until a new job; ESOPs keep vesting; health insurance continues; outplacement help; agency staff get two months, not 15 days [1][3].
- What happened next: Not reported in our sources (how many found jobs is unknown).
- Tests: 7.2.1 (let the wrong hire go early, and kindly). **Only half supported.** The "kindly" part is strongly sourced [1]. But these were not wrong hires: the company said there was not enough work for them [1]. The draft should test "how to let people go kindly", not "spot the wrong hire".

### D4 · May–July 2020 · Who takes pay cuts, and how much?
- Situation: Voluntary cuts fell short of the goal [1].
- Options: (a) the same flat cut for everyone; (b) a sliding cut: small for low salaries, up to half for the highest, with shares (ESOPs) in return; (c) no pay cuts; cut more jobs instead.
- What they did: (b) from June [1][2]. Once business picked up, all salaries were restored in July [9][10]. One secondary source says lost pay was paid back three times over [12] (unconfirmed).
- What happened next: 75% of staff had taken partial cuts, saving 14% of payroll [9][13].
- Tests: 7.2.3 (when cash is short, protect your people's pay first). **Partly supported.** The sliding scale protected low earners [1], and pay came back within weeks [9]. But Zomato did cut pay across the company, and no opened source says what the founders took in 2020 (the CEO's salary waiver only starts in April 2021 [17]). Don't claim leaders cut their own pay first.

### D5 · April–June 2020 · A fast test of grocery delivery
- Situation: Food orders down 70% [5]; people need essentials at home; delivery riders have little work [5][7].
- Options: (a) stick to food only and wait for restaurants; (b) launch grocery fast with partners, in many cities, and be ready to stop; (c) go all in on grocery: warehouses, own stock, a new brand.
- What they did: (b). Grocery in 80+ cities by 8 April [7], 185 by mid-May, with partners like Grofers [1][8].
- What happened next: When restaurants reopened (8 June) Zomato scaled grocery back [8]; it ended by July [14][15]. A second try in 2021 also ended, and Zomato bought a stake in Grofers instead [15].
- Tests: 1.3.2 (test small before you bet big). **Supported, with an honest twist:** the test was fast and cheap to stop, but not small (80+ cities). The idea failed; the lesson is that a test you can stop is still a good call.

## What really happened
The lockdown cut Zomato's orders by most of their volume within days [5][9]. In March staff took voluntary pay cuts and the company set up funds for riders and daily wage workers [4][7]. It tried grocery delivery within two weeks [7]. On 15 May it let go about 13% of staff with six months' half pay, and cut everyone else's pay on a sliding scale [1][2]. Restaurants reopened in June; Zomato pulled back from grocery [8]. By July food delivery was at 60% of normal, burn was near zero, and all salaries were restored [9][10]. A year later, in July 2021, Zomato listed on the stock market and its shares rose about two-thirds on the first day [16]. Suggested `realEnding`: legend.

## Risks
- **Living people (main risk).** Every leader is living and the layoffs hurt real people. Follow the curriculum rule: only what the company did and announced, no motives, no quotes, no faces next to lines about others.
- **Litigation.** Not researched for this brief. The company is large and listed and may face cases (regulators, tax, gig-worker issues). None is about the 2020 layoffs in our sources. Check before publishing.
- **Conflicting numbers.** Headcount: about 4,000 staff [2] vs about 5,000 [11]; laid off: 13% [1], more than 500 [2], 520 [12][13]. Use "about one in eight staff". OfficeChai dates the layoffs to March [13]; the right date is 15 May [1][2].
- **Payback claim.** The "three times the lost pay" story comes only from [12], a secondary source. Leave it out unless the original Zomato post or tweet is found.
- **Grocery end date.** The company said in June it would scale back, not close [8]; Wikipedia says it ran until July [14]; MediaNama says it ended "within a few months" [15]. Write "Zomato soon pulled back from grocery", not a close date.
- **Name.** The company later renamed itself (seen only in search results; not opened). Use "Zomato", the 2020 name.
