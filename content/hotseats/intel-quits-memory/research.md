## Timeline
- 1970: Intel's 1103, a 1,024-bit DRAM priced at about 1 cent per bit, becomes the first chip to seriously challenge magnetic core memory [9][1].
- 1978: Intel's Fab 5 in Oregon opens, making mainly memory chips [7].
- 1981: IBM launches its PC with Intel's 8088 inside [3].
- 1981–82: IBM's buyers insist on a second source; Intel signs AMD as a second source for its PC processors [3].
- Early 1980s: Japanese makers NEC, Hitachi and Fujitsu reach yields up to 40% higher than US makers, at lower cost [1].
- 1982: Work begins on the 386 [11].
- 1982-12: IBM pays $250 million ($40 a share) for 12% of Intel; Intel says the money will go to R&D and more production capacity [10].
- 1983: IBM gets rights to make some Intel chips itself as its own internal second source [3]. Memory is still Intel's largest source of revenue [7].
- 1983–84: Chip demand booms with the PC; Intel licenses other makers [4].
- 1984-Q4: The boom collapses, causing too much capacity and price falls in 1985 [4]. Intel's top managers face the question of leaving DRAM [6].
- 1984: Intel's capital spending is $388 million, up 168% from 1983; its profit of $198 million is a record [4][5].
- 1980s: Japan's share of world DRAM production rises to 90% [8].
- 1985: Intel leaves DRAM. Its farewell letter says 256K DRAMs sell for under $3.00 and losses will continue. DRAM is under 5% of Intel's business [1]. Intel blames Japanese below-cost selling [7].
- 1985: Intel has a sharp fall in sales and the biggest loss in its history so far [2].
- 1985-10: First 386 samples are released [11]; the 386 took four years and $100 million to build [2].
- 1985–86: Intel closes eight older, smaller plants and cuts its workforce by 7,200 through attrition and layoffs [4]. It still spends $210 million on equipment [4].
- 1986: R&D is $228 million, 18% of revenue, up $33 million [4]. Intel decides to sell its bubble memory business and keeps EPROMs [4].
- 1986: The US and Japan sign a semiconductor trade agreement; the ITC upholds Intel's complaint on EPROM dumping [4].
- 1986: Intel decides not to license the 386 to AMD or others, and makes it in two of its own fabs [3][2].
- 1986-06: 386 mass production begins [11].
- 1986-09-09: Compaq announces the Deskpro 386, the first PC with the 386 [12].
- 1986: Intel loses $173 million on revenue of $1,260 million [5].
- 1987: The CEO post passes from the long-time chief to the company president [13].
- 1987-07: IBM's first 386 PC ships, nearly a year after Compaq's [12].
- 1987-12: 386 supply is far behind demand; Intel expects 1987 revenue of $1,800 million and profit well above the 1984 record [5].
- 1991-03: AMD's Am386 arrives after legal fights; the 386 had been Intel-only for 4.7 years [11].
- 1992: Intel has 83% of the microprocessor market and over $1 billion in yearly profit [2].

## Decision points
### D1 · 1982 · IBM wants to buy a piece of Intel
- Situation: The IBM PC, launched in 1981 with Intel's 8088, is selling far better than expected [3]. Intel needs money for research and factories [10].
- Options (3): (a) accept IBM's money and spend it on research and new capacity; (b) refuse and stay fully independent; (c) take the money and pour it into memory chips to fight Japanese makers.
- What the company did: Intel sold IBM 12% of its shares for $250 million and said it would use the money for R&D and production capacity [10].
- What happened next: IBM became Intel's biggest customer [2]; IBM also gained rights to make some Intel chips itself (1983) [3].

### D2 · 1984 · The memory price war
- Situation: Japanese rivals have yields up to 40% higher and lower costs [1]. DRAMs have been a minor part of Intel's product mix for five years, while microprocessors became the core [6]. The chip boom collapses in late 1984 [4].
- Options (3): (a) build a big new memory factory to match Japan on cost; (b) keep making DRAMs at small scale and wait for prices to recover; (c) leave DRAM now and move factories to microprocessors.
- What the company did: Intel stayed in DRAM through 1984 into 1985 [1][6].
- What happened next: Prices kept falling in 1985; 256K DRAMs sold for under $3.00 and Intel expected substantial losses [1][4].

### D3 · 1985 · Leave the business you started?
- Situation: Intel made the first big-selling DRAM in 1970 [9]. Japan now makes most of the world's DRAMs [8]. DRAM is under 5% of Intel's business [1].
- Options (3): (a) stay and ask the government for trade protection; (b) leave all memory, including EPROMs; (c) leave DRAM but keep EPROMs, where Intel is strong.
- What the company did: Intel left DRAM in 1985 [1][8] and kept EPROMs, gaining EPROM share in 1985 and 1986 [4]. It did also file a dumping complaint on EPROMs, which the ITC upheld [4].
- What happened next: Intel focused on microprocessors [13]; it sold bubble memory in 1986 [4].

### D4 · 1985 · Too big for the business you have
- Situation: Intel's costs were built for a $2–3 billion company, but it was becoming a $1–1.5 billion one [4]. 1985 brought the biggest loss in its history so far [2].
- Options (3): (a) cut research to save jobs; (b) cut pay a little and keep every plant; (c) close older plants and cut staff, but keep spending on research and new equipment.
- What the company did: Over 1985–86 Intel closed eight older, smaller plants and cut its workforce by 7,200 [4], while spending $210 million on equipment and raising R&D to $228 million in 1986 [4].
- What happened next: Manufacturing productivity doubled in two years [4]; 1986 was still a loss year of $173 million [5].

### D5 · 1986 · Share the 386 or keep it?
- Situation: The 386 cost four years and $100 million [2]. Custom said a second maker must also build each chip; AMD had done this for earlier chips [2][3]. AMD had not delivered products Intel wanted in exchange [3].
- Options (3): (a) license the 386 to AMD as before; (b) license it to many makers so it spreads fast; (c) be the only maker, and reassure buyers by building it in two of your own fabs.
- What the company did: Intel did not license the 386, after a heated internal debate, and made it in two of its own fabs [3][2].
- What happened next: The 386 was Intel-only for 4.7 years; AMD's version came in March 1991 after legal fights [11].

### D6 · 1986 · IBM says no to the 386
- Situation: IBM, Intel's biggest customer, was not interested in the 386 and was building its own version of the older 286 [2][3].
- Options (3): (a) wait until IBM is ready; (b) sell the 386 to anyone with no special partner; (c) work closely with Compaq and Microsoft to launch the first 386 PC.
- What the company did: Intel, Microsoft and Compaq worked together on the Compaq Deskpro 386 [3], announced on 9 September 1986 [12].
- What happened next: IBM's first 386 PC came in July 1987 [12]. By late 1987 386 supply was far behind demand [5]. By 1992 Intel had 83% of the microprocessor market [2].

## What really happened
Intel started as a memory company and made the first big-selling DRAM in 1970 [9]. In the early 1980s Japanese makers beat it on yield and cost [1], and Japan came to make most of the world's DRAMs [8]. In 1985 Intel left DRAM [1]. Over 1985–86 it closed eight plants and cut 7,200 jobs [4] and lost $173 million in 1986 [5]. It bet on the 386, kept it to itself instead of licensing it [3], and launched it with Compaq in 1986 [12]. By late 1987 it expected record profits [5], and by 1992 it had 83% of the microprocessor market [2].

## Risks
- Litigation today: none central. The Intel–AMD fights over the 386 were in the past (Am386 arrived 1991) [11]; the hot seat only mentions "legal fights" in history.
- Living people: the two CEOs are no longer living; panel members are living but only named here. hotseat.json uses roles only.
- Thin sources: Intel's own annual-report pages redirect and could not be opened; the 1986 letter facts come from a Substack summary [4]. Revenue fall and the exact 1985 loss are not given in an opened source, so the story says only "biggest loss in its history so far" [2].
- Conflicting: one search snippet put 1986 job cuts at "some 8,000"; we use 7,200 over two years from the 1986 letter [4]. Some sources say Intel's DRAM share was about 1% by 1984 (not opened).
- Motives: why AMD was left out is disputed (AMD said it was betrayed) [3]; the story states only the decision.
