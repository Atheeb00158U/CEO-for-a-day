## Timeline
- 1984: Titan Watches formed as a joint venture of the Tata group and TIDCO (Tamil Nadu) to make quartz watches [1].
- 1990–91: India's foreign exchange crisis; RBI pledges 47 tonnes of gold; companies are pushed to earn their own foreign exchange [1][2].
- 1990: the Titan MD visits a jewellery exhibition in Mumbai; the idea of a jewellery business is born [1].
- 1992-08: pilot plant set up [2][5].
- 1993: Titan shows its jewellery at INHORGENTA in Munich and the Basel fair; buyers are not convinced [1]. Tata Sons approves a Rs 65 crore jewellery export plant in Hosur, Tamil Nadu [1].
- By 1993-03: Titan has 60% of India's quartz analogue watch market [1].
- 1994: the Hosur plant starts; export operations sputter and the export opportunity turns out not to exist [1][2].
- 1994–95: Titan turns to India. Its market research head suggests testing whether Indians would buy 18-karat instead of 22-karat; no research is done [1].
- 1995: Tanishq launch ad campaign; jewellery sold through other jewellers and Titan watch showrooms [1].
- 1996-07: first Tanishq store opens on Cathedral Road, Chennai: a European-style boutique, all 18-karat diamond jewellery, visits by appointment [1].
- 1996–97: walk-ins low; the team gets 22-karat jewellery made by makers in Mumbai and Mysore; the Chennai store closes its first year at Rs 2.8 crore of sales against a Rs 3.5 crore target [1]. Tanishq sales Rs 32 crore, loss Rs 10.60 crore [2].
- 1996: Tanishq's head of manufacturing sees an X-ray fluorescence (XRF) machine at the Basel show [1].
- 1996–97 annual report: Titan admits a "very strong" Indian preference for 22-karat and says buyers often get under-karat jewellery sold as 22-karat [1].
- 1997: a new jewellery head brings more traditional 22-karat lines with ad support, and shows gold weight, diamond details and making charges separately [1]. XRF machines (over Rs 10 lakh each) go into boutiques nationwide; Lintas brands it the "Karatmeter" [1].
- 1997 onward: queues of people test their gold; many find it is below 22 karat; local jewellers lean on Tanishq franchisees, some threaten violence [1]. Testing is free, including for gold bought elsewhere [7][9][12].
- 1997–98: sales Rs 38 crore, loss Rs 21.96 crore; 1998–99: sales Rs 73 crore, loss Rs 10.40 crore; 1999–2000: sales Rs 141 crore [2].
- FY 2000: Titan's profit margin falls to 3.4% (8.9% in FY 1995); market value Rs 311 crore in May 2000 (Rs 554 crore in May 1995) [1].
- 2000-05: a new MD is named; the COO resigns the next day with six senior executives; the entire sales and marketing team quits [2].
- 2000: 30 boutiques. Surveys show buyers don't know the brand, find it too expensive, or feel it isn't for people like them; loud ads aren't working [2].
- 2000–01: fifth-anniversary offer draws big crowds; ads show specific products; a range from Rs 399; collections for working women; corporate sales [2]. First profit, Rs 2.07 crore, on sales of Rs 188 crore [2].
- 2001–02: sales Rs 299 crore, profit Rs 1.87 crore [2].
- 2002: Indian gold demand falls 19%, from 843 to 680 tonnes [2].
- Early 2000s: "Impure to Pure" exchange: jewellery testing 19–22 karat swapped for 22-karat Tanishq jewellery; the buyer pays only making charges and Tanishq bears the gold cost [4][7][8].
- 2002–03: retail sales Rs 332 crore (+39%), total Rs 389 crore; net profit up 318% to Rs 7.82 crore; 60 stores in 47 towns; 1.2 million women shopped; "Impure to Pure" credited with winning customers from traditional jewellers [2][3]. Tanishq is 43% of Titan's business [2].
- 2003: Tanishq is among India's top five retailers [5].
- 2008: 105 stores in 71 cities [5].
- FY25: Titan's jewellery division total income above ₹46,000 crore [11].

## Decision points
### D1 · 1994–95 · What to do with a jewellery plant built for Europe
- Situation: The Rs 65 crore Hosur plant was built to make minimal 18-karat jewellery for Europe and America, but the export plan failed [1]. Indian buyers preferred 22-karat (91.6% gold), saw jewellery as a store of value, and over two-thirds of jewellery made in the early 1990s was wedding jewellery [1]. The market research head suggested testing whether Indians would buy 18-karat [1].
- Options: (a) launch in India with 18-karat Western diamond jewellery in European-style boutiques; (b) research Indian buyers first and lead with 22-karat gold; (c) mix: Western 18-karat plus a small 22-karat line.
- What the company did: (a). No research was done; the brand would offer more diamonds than gold, in boutiques modelled on Europe, the "Tiffany of the East" [1].
- What happened next: the first store (Chennai, July 1996) had low walk-ins most of its first year [1]; 1996–97 loss Rs 10.60 crore [2].

### D2 · 1996 · The first store is empty
- Situation: Chennai store feedback: "we need 22-karat jewellery" [1].
- Options: (a) quickly add 22-karat pieces but keep 18-karat diamond as the main range; (b) hold firm and teach buyers 18-karat; (c) make 22-karat gold the main range and make stores welcoming.
- What the company did: (a). 22-karat pieces made by makers in Mumbai and Mysore; walk-ins improved a lot, but most lines stayed 18-karat diamond [1].
- What happened next: first-year Chennai sales Rs 2.8 crore vs Rs 3.5 crore target [1]; 1996–97 annual report admits the 22-karat preference [1]; more 22-karat lines from 1997 [1].

### D3 · 1997 · The X-ray machine
- Situation: Under-karat jewellery was rampant; buyers paid for 22 karat and got 19 or 20 [1]. Proving it meant melting the jewellery [1]. An XRF machine could read gold content in about three minutes without harm [1]; each cost more than Rs 10 lakh while the business was losing money [1].
- Options: (a) put machines in stores and test anyone's gold free; (b) keep it in the lab for your own quality checks; (c) skip it to save money.
- What the company did: (a). Machines in boutiques nationwide from 1997, branded "Karatmeter" by Lintas [1]; testing free even for gold bought elsewhere [7][9].
- What happened next: queues of hundreds; buyers shocked and angry at their jewellers; local jewellers pressured franchisees, some threatened violence [1]. Purity ads aimed directly at family jewellers' practices became the brand story [1]. Losses continued: Rs 21.96 crore (1997–98), Rs 10.40 crore (1998–99), but sales rose to Rs 141 crore by 1999–2000 [2].

### D4 · 2000 · The team walks out, the stores are quiet
- Situation: new MD in May 2000; COO quits with six seniors; whole sales and marketing team resigns [2]. Loud ads not working; buyers don't know the brand, think it costly, or "not for people like me" [2]. Titan's margin down to 3.4% [1].
- Options: (a) bring people in with a fifth-birthday offer, product-and-price ads and a range from Rs 399; (b) spend even more on glamorous brand ads; (c) close weak stores and shrink to a few big cities.
- What the company did: (a), plus collections for working women and corporate sales [2].
- What happened next: big crowds; first profit Rs 2.07 crore on Rs 188 crore sales in 2000–01 [2].

### D5 · early 2000s · Turn shock into sales
- Situation: the Karatmeter had shown many families their gold was below 22 karat [1].
- Options: (a) swap any 19–22 karat jewellery for 22-karat Tanishq jewellery, the buyer paying only making charges; (b) buy old gold at its true tested value only; (c) stop free testing to make peace with jewellers.
- What the company did: (a), "Impure to Pure" [3][4][7].
- What happened next: credited with winning customers from traditional jewellers; 2002–03 retail sales +39%, net profit +318% [3]; 43% of Titan's business [2].

## What really happened
Tanishq began as an export project born of India's forex crisis [1][2]. It launched in India with Western 18-karat diamond jewellery and lost money for five straight years [1][2]. It moved toward 22-karat gold, and from 1997 used the Karatmeter to let anyone test their gold, exposing widespread under-karat jewellery [1]. After a leadership crisis in 2000, a new team brought customers in and made the first profit in 2000–01 [2]. The "Impure to Pure" exchange won customers from traditional jewellers, and by 2002–03 Tanishq was 43% of Titan's business [2][3]. By 2003 it was among India's top five retailers [5], and it became India's largest jewellery brand [1][11].

## Risks
- Living people: most of the team (Bhat, Kurian, Nangia, Saldanha, Balki, Kant, Venkataraman) are living. The story uses roles only; the founder MD died in 2016. The founder's private views come from [1]'s colleagues' recollections; hotseat.json does not state them as fact.
- Litigation: none active found. A 2002 Economic Times headline (seen only as a title cited in [11]) says Tanishq was asked to reply to unfair trade practice charges; not opened, historical, outcome unknown. The 2020 "Ekatvam" ad row is outside this story.
- A 2026 TV series ("Made In India: A Titan Story") dramatises this period [6]; players may have seen a fictional version.
- Thin/conflicting sources: Karatmeter start year is 1997 in [1] but 1999 in several secondary pieces (ICMR summaries, [6]); we use [1]. "Impure to Pure" launch date not confirmed from an opened source. Accumulated loss "Rs 150 crore" appears only in secondary sources [8][14]; [2]'s table sums to about Rs 43 crore for the three listed loss years. MD change: [2] says May 2000, [6] says Desai retired 2002. The "~60% of tested pieces impure" figure is unsourced and not used.
- Several secondary sources (Buildd, Inventiva, Equentis, blogs) repeat each other; real-path text relies mainly on [1], [2], [3].
