# Eureka Forbes: research brief
Hot Seat id: eureka-forbes-door-to-door · Years covered: 1982–2014 (epilogue to 2025) · Researched: 2026-10-30

**Big warning first.** Good sources on the 1980s and 1990s are thin. Most hard numbers come from 2005, 2012 and 2014 press pieces. No opened source gives an early price, the cost of one sale, or a written plan for which homes to visit. See "Lesson coverage" and "Open questions".

## Decision points
### D1 · 1982 · Shops, or a demo in every home?
- Situation: A new company with a product most Indian homes had never used (a vacuum cleaner), and soon a water purifier [3][6][7].
- Options: (a) sell through appliance shops and dealers, with ads; (b) hire young salespeople to show the product working inside people's homes; (c) both: a few showrooms plus some home demos.
- What they did: (b). In-home demonstrations, no shops, for over 20 years [3][6].
- What happened next: It grew from one Mumbai office with 20 reps (1987) to 590 towns (2012) [2]. "Aquaguard" became the everyday name for a purifier [3][9].
- Tests: 5.1.2 (ask questions first, then listen). **Partly supported.** Sources prove home demos, and training in "demonstration" [3], but none describes the reps asking questions before the pitch. The draft can show the demo; the "ask first" part would be our framing.

### D2 · late 1980s · Which homes to knock on first?
- Situation: Twenty reps in one city office [2]; a costly product [3][7].
- Options: (a) every door in every street; (b) homes that can pay and have a reason to care, such as families with small children; (c) offices and shops first, homes later.
- What they did: Per one weak source, early sales aimed at families with young children [10].
- What happened next: Not documented in a solid source.
- Tests: 5.1.1 (sell to the person who needs it, can pay, and decides). **Not supported by a solid source.** Only [10], a marketing blog, says who was targeted. Either find a better source or make this decision a pure alternate-history call and label it in the draft notes.

### D3 · 1984 onward · "Too costly. We boil water for free."
- Situation: Boiling was how homes cleaned water [3][10]. Aquaguard was dear: about US$175 even in 2005 [3].
- Options: (a) cut the price to win more homes; (b) show what boiled water still lacks and what illness costs, with a live demo (the "compared to what?" answer); (c) give up on purifiers and push vacuum cleaners.
- What they did: (b), in spirit. The company persuaded many Indians to stop boiling tap water and buy Aquaguard [3]. One weak source says demos showed dirty-looking liquid coming out clean [10].
- What happened next: Aquaguard reached 75% of the market by 2004–05 [2][3].
- Tests: 5.1.3 (when they say "too costly", ask "compared to what?"). **Partly supported.** The outcome (people stopped boiling) is solid [3]; the exact sales script is not sourced.

### D4 · 1980s–2000s · After the sale: service visits, or the next door?
- Situation: A purifier needs installing and new filters. Each salesperson is paid mostly on new sales [3].
- Options: (a) move on to the next home, and let local mechanics fix machines; (b) install every machine and send technicians back for regular service, and sell service plans; (c) charge for every visit, only when the customer calls.
- What they did: (b). A company plumber installed each purifier and technicians came back for maintenance [3]; water-testing labs [3][5]; paid service plans (AMC) [7][10].
- What happened next: By 2012, close to 9 million customer homes [2]; by FY23, service was nearly 30% of sales [8]. But only about a quarter of machines are on a service plan [7][9], and some customers complain about plans not delivered [1].
- Tests: 5.2.4 (a customer is worth the margin on all future buys), +5.1.5 (follow up). **Supported for 5.2.4** (service and filters as repeat income [7][8]). 5.1.5 is only implied (repeat visits); no source on following up a "no".
- Note: When service plans started is not sourced.

### D5 · 2005–2014 · What does one sale cost through a sales team?
- Situation: 5,300 reps in 2005 [3], about 7,500 in 2012 [2], over 8,000 in 2014 [4]. Pay is 60% commission, plus training and motorcycles [3]. Gated buildings make cold calls harder [4]; rivals sell in shops [4].
- Options: (a) keep the door-to-door team as the core and add online leads for demos; (b) cut the team and move to shops; (c) hire more reps to knock on more doors.
- What they did: (a) in 2014: the company said it would not disband its sales force, and used digital ads to get demo requests [2][4].
- What happened next: Market share fell from 75% (2004) to 60% (2012) [2]. Under new owners, shops and online grew; direct sales are about a quarter of sales by 2025 [6][9].
- Tests: 5.2.3 (know what it costs to win one customer), +5.2.5 (each customer must earn more margin than they cost). **Partly supported.** We have the pay structure [3] and doors knocked [2], but no source gives a cost per sale or margin per machine. Any sum in the draft must be clearly made up ("say a rep earns ₹X…"), not stated as Eureka Forbes data.
- Quiz only: 5.1.4, 5.2.1, 5.2.2. 5.2.1 (master one channel first) fits D1/D5 well and could be tested here if wanted.

## What really happened
Eureka Forbes started in 1982 selling vacuum cleaners in homes, not shops [3][6]. It added Aquaguard, India's first water purifier, in the mid-1980s [7]. From one Mumbai office with 20 reps in 1987, its door-to-door team grew to over 8,000 and reached about 9 million homes [2][4]. It talked India out of boiling water, and Aquaguard held 75% of the market in 2004–05 [2][3]. It installed and serviced each machine, and service became a big, steady income [3][8]. Later, gated buildings, cheaper RO rivals and shops eroded its lead [2][4][7]. New owners since 2021–22 sell through shops and online as well; direct sales are now about a quarter of sales [6][9]. Suggested `realEnding`: survivor (strong for 25 years, then lost ground), for Atheeb to decide.

## Risks
- **Thin sources (main risk).** The 1982–1995 years rest on a few lines in [2], [3] and [6]. Decision D2 has no solid source; D1, D3 and D5 are only partly supported (see each). Customer acquisition cost is never reported.
- **Litigation.** Eureka Forbes sued Midea in the Delhi High Court in April 2026 over the "Eureka" name [6] (blog source; confirm). It also had ad and legal fights with Hindustan Unilever around 2013 [5], and consumer-forum complaints about service plans [1]. None is about the 1980s story, but the company is in active litigation, so flag to Atheeb.
- **Living people.** The 1987–2014 leader and later CEOs are or may be living. Keep them as roles; no claims about motives.
- **Conflicting facts.** Aquaguard year: 1984 [7][10] vs 1986 [6] vs about 1988 [5]. Founding partner name: Forbes & Company [1], Forbes Gokak [3], Forbes & Campbell [7] (all Tata-linked Forbes companies). Sales force: 5,300 (2005) [3], 7,500 (2012) [2], 8,000+ (2014) [4], 5,500 frontline (2025) [9].
- **Brand and logo.** "Aquaguard" and "Eurochamp" are trademarks; no logos in images.
- **Company-flattering sources.** [2], [3] and [4] lean on interviews with the company's leaders.
